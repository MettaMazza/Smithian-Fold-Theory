# The Harmonics of the Integers — Number Theory as Fold Dynamics

Number theory has always looked like a museum of separate facts: the order of two

The fold map is doubling: a value p/q goes to 2p/q, with whole Ones removed to keep

## The orbit-length law — the integers' periods are the fold's periods

Take any reduced fraction p/q with q odd. Fold it over and over. It returns to where
it began after a fixed number of steps, and that number is the **multiplicative
order of two modulo q** — and it does not depend on p. Every reduced fraction with
the same odd denominator shares one period. A whole denominator-class is a single
eternal mode with a single frequency.

This was checked directly against the fold engine's own orbit counter across a
hundred denominators: the live period and the number-theoretic order matched every
time. The period of the repeating *binary* expansion of 1/q — a fact from the
arithmetic of decimals — is literally the length of the fold orbit of 1/q. The two
were never different. The repeating expansion *is* the eternal orbit, written out.

## The orbit count — eternal modes per denominator

The reduced fractions of a given odd denominator do not all sit in one orbit. They

## The antipodal involution — and the seed of the critical line

## Eternal and transient — where mass lives in the integers

Odd denominators are eternal: pure cycles, never decaying. Even denominators decay.
A denominator two-to-the-a times an odd core folds *downward* for exactly a steps —
the transient, the shortfall, the **mass** — and then locks forever into the eternal
cycle of its odd core. The two-adic valuation of the denominator is the lifetime of
the transient. Mass, in the fold's physics, is precisely this decaying part, and
here it is again in the raw integers: the power of two in the denominator is how
many steps a number falls before it becomes eternal.

## The fully-ergodic denominators — Artin, renamed

A prime denominator can hold a single orbit that visits *every* nonzero residue —
the maximal eternal mode, length one less than the prime. This happens exactly when
two is a primitive root modulo that prime. The fold renames the old question: "is
two a primitive root mod q" becomes "is q a single fully-ergodic fold mode." Across
the first several hundred primes the density of these fully-ergodic denominators

conjectured limiting density of primes with two as a primitive root. The fold did
not need to assume Artin's conjecture; it produced the same population by counting
which denominators carry one maximal orbit.
