# The Mass Chord — the three lepton masses are roots of one forced cubic

There are three copies of the electron. The electron, the muon, the tau — same charge, same everything, except the muon is about two hundred and seven times heavier, and the tau heavier again. When the muon turned up, the physicist Rabi famously asked, "who ordered that?" — and the honest answer from consensus, to this day, is: nobody knows. The masses are free parameters. They are measured, written down by hand, and left unexplained, three of the couple-dozen knobs the Standard Model can't account for. The fold accounts for them — not as three numbers, but as one chord.

## Three notes, one equation

In the fold the three charged leptons are not three independent things. They are three roots of a single cubic equation — one polynomial, struck once, ringing three notes. Here it is:

lambda-cubed, minus lambda-squared, plus one-sixth lambda, minus one-over-four-hundred-and-eighty-five, equals zero.

In symbols: λ³ − λ² + (1/6)λ − 1/485 = 0. Three coefficients, and every one of them is forced by structure, not chosen. The one-sixth is one over two-times-three. The one-over-four-eighty-five is one over two-times-three-to-the-fifth, minus one — four-eighty-six minus one. Those are not fitted constants; they are the same family rule that runs through the whole mass sector, evaluated at the third generation. One label — the number three — and the coefficients fall out, and the cubic has exactly three roots, and those three roots are the three leptons. You don't get a fourth, because a cubic has three roots. Three generations, sealed, for the same reason the equation has the degree it has.

And the three sit at clean positions in the domain — one-sixth, one-half, five-sixths — with the muon parked exactly on the half, the Still Point, the self-mirror at the centre. The middle note of the chord is the one that sits on the calm centre of everything.

## The number that fell out

So what does the chord actually predict? Take the ratio of the muon's mass to the electron's. Out of that cubic, with nothing tuned, it comes to about two hundred and seven — 207.09. The measured ratio is 206.77. A bare rational, forced from a one-label rule, landing on the measured muon-to-electron mass ratio to better than two parts in a thousand. Consensus types that ratio in by hand to as many digits as you like and calls it an input. The fold *derives* it, to three figures, from a polynomial it wasn't allowed to adjust.

## The muon's other fame — g−2

The muon has a second claim to fame: its anomalous magnetic moment, "g minus two," the measurement at Fermilab that keeps almost-but-not-quite disagreeing with theory and sending everyone into a flap about new physics. The fold has a clean word on it. The leading piece of that anomaly is set by the fine-structure constant — the very same alpha, two-fifty over thirty-four-thousand-two-fifty-nine, that we counted in an earlier video. And the *excess* — the part that scales with the particle — goes as the **square of the lepton mass ratio.** That is exactly why the muon is so spectacularly sensitive to g−2 and the electron is nearly deaf to it: two hundred and seven, squared, is about forty-three thousand. The muon feels the anomaly some forty-three thousand times more strongly than the electron, because that's what squaring the mass ratio does. The sensitivity isn't a mystery; it's mass-squared, forced.
