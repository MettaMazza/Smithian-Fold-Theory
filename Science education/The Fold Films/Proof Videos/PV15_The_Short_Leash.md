# The Short Leash — why the weak force barely reaches, and why there's a smallest lump

Light from a galaxy crosses the entire observable universe to land in your eye. The weak force — the one that runs radioactive decay and lights the sun's first step — reaches about a thousandth of the width of a single proton, and then it is simply gone. Same cosmos, two forces, and a gap in their reach of forty-odd orders of magnitude. Why does one carry forever and the other die inside a speck? And — the same question wearing a different coat — why is there a *smallest possible lump* of the strong field, instead of ever-fainter massless ones? Consensus calls the second of these the Yang-Mills mass gap, has hung a million-dollar prize on proving it, and considers it gloriously open. Both questions have one answer, and it is about mass and the floor beneath it.

## Reach is a leash, and mass holds it

Picture a force spreading outward as a running intensity. If its mediator is **massless**, it subtracts nothing as it travels — the intensity rolls on undiminished, and the reach is *unbounded* (`verify_weak_range`: the massless mediator's reach is unbounded). That is light. That is why a star is visible across the void. But if the mediator carries a **mass**, then at every step the mass takes its cut — the intensity is docked the mediator's mass-part each stride, and after a finite number of strides there is nothing left to spend. The reach is *finite* (`verify_weak_range`: a mediator of mass one-third has a reach of just two strides). The heavier the mediator, the sooner it runs out of road. Reach falls as one over the mass. The weak force is not feeble — strike it up close and it is mighty — it is *leashed.* Its carriers are heavy, the heaviest force-carriers there are, so the leash is brutally short and the force can only touch what is almost on top of it. Weakness, here, is not a small strength. It is a short reach.

## The floor under the lightest lump

So if a heavy carrier means a short leash, could a confined sector simply produce a *massless* lump — a strong excitation with no mass, and therefore an infinite leash? It cannot, and the reason is the oldest rule in the fold. The lightest excitation of the strong field — the mass gap — is forced to **one-third** (`verify_yang_mills_mass_gap`). And one-third is no arbitrary floor: it is the **Mirror of the strong self-coupling, two-thirds** — the two of them summing to exactly the One, a closed period-two orbit under the fold, each folding into the other, their average one-half folding home to unison. The coupling is two-thirds; its complement, the gap, is one-third; together, the One. There is no massless glueball because a massless lump would be a *zero* sitting in the spectrum — and zero is forbidden, thrown out of the domain on the first page. The spectrum is not allowed to start at nothing, so it must start at a gap. **The mass gap is the No-Zero floor, written into the masses.** The same rule that caught the falling boy and left the residue of matter also forbids the strong field its weightless, infinite-reach lump — and fixes the smallest one it is allowed at one-third, the coupling's own Mirror.

## Two faces of one floor

So the two questions were one. Reach is a leash held by mass: massless carriers reach forever, massive carriers are short-leashed, and the weak force is the most leashed of all because its carriers are the heaviest. And mass itself has a floor, because nothing can be nothing: there is no massless strong lump, no zero in the spectrum, so even a confined sector's longest possible leash is finite, anchored to a smallest mass of one-third. The weak force barely reaches because its messengers are heavy; the strong field always has a smallest lump because it is never allowed a weightless one. Heaviness shortens the leash; the floor forbids the leash from ever being infinite where a mass must exist. One rule, two faces. Every step traces to the One; zero free parameters.
