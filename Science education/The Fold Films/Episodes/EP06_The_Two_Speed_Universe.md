# Ep 06 — The Two-Speed Universe

Right — housekeeping, for the one. Everything coming up about the actual maths, the actual cosmology, and the real history is true — documented, it's the fold or it's the record or it's what the consensus mob themselves admit. Everything else — me, the fire, the crew, the gowns, and the whole expanding country I'm about to walk them through — is autofiction. And the pictures are AI, fitted to the script and never accurate to the maths or the story. Made up, to tell you something true. You knew that. Course you did. But one fella in the back genuinely needed that spelling out, so now it's spelled. Hello. Sit down.

## Two tape measures, one row

Ordinary evening, and a domestic has broken out. Errol and Nadia are both trying to measure the same thing — the distance from the fire to the old dead tree — and they have got two different answers, and neither will budge.

Nadia, brandishing her count, says — *"forty-one. I paced it, heel to toe, all the way. Forty-one."*

Errol, not even slightly bothered, says — *"forty-four. I sighted it off the tree and the far ridge, the way you measure a thing you can't be arsed to walk. Forty-four."*

And it should be the easiest thing in the world to settle — it's a *distance*, it's a tree, you'd think two grown adults could agree how far away a tree is. But Nadia trusts her feet and Errol trusts his eye, and feet and eye have come back with two different numbers, and now it isn't about the tree any more, it's about *whose method is sound* — which is the exact moment any measurement stops being about the thing and starts being about the people. Matthew tried to broker a peace by pacing it himself and got a *third* number, which helped nobody. Sol measured it with a bit of string and got Nadia's. Danny measured it by lying down end over end across the grass, lost count somewhere past thirty, and had to start again.

And they go again, and they get the same two numbers again, because they are *both careful* and they are *both right* and the numbers *still don't match*, and Wee Danny, watching them like tennis, finally says the whole episode out loud — *"hang on. You're both dead careful. You both got the same answer twice. But they're different. So who's wrong? Or — can you both be right and it still not add up?"*

## The greatest crisis in cosmology

And Team Tenure, who can smell an unresolved measurement at a great distance, descended — and for once they weren't dismissive, they were *thrilled*, in the worst way.

The Dean swept in almost glowing and announced — *"ahh, you've stumbled onto the Hubble tension! The single greatest crisis in modern cosmology!"* And he explained, with relish, that the universe's expansion comes out at one speed when you measure it from the ancient afterglow of the beginning, and a *different* speed when you measure it from the exploding stars nearby, and the two will not reconcile, and this *"may mean physics itself is broken,"* and required, he said, eyes shining, *"a vast and ongoing programme of work."* And then, the tell — *"though of course it's far beyond anyone without the training. Best leave the broken universe to us."*

A week ago a "broken universe" might've rattled them. But the Gardener had watched a monster turn to smoke just for being looked at, and she narrowed her eyes — *"two careful measurements that disagree. We've literally got that exact row happening over a tree. I don't think your universe is broken. I think you've not found the step between them. Come on."*

## The tree that never got closer

And the way in, this time, was the argument itself. They decided to settle the tree once and for all by walking it *together* — the whole crew, counting out loud in step — and they set off across the grass toward the old dead tree. And they walked. And the tree did not get closer. They walked further, counting past forty, past fifty, and it sat out there on the horizon exactly as far off as when they'd started — because the ground between them and it had quietly begun to *grow*, paying out fresh grass under their feet faster than their legs could eat it up. By the time Danny thought to look back, the fire was a single spark a mile behind them, and there was no easy evening stroll to any tree anywhere; they'd walked clean out of the ordinary world and into a country that would not hold still. Because this one was *expanding*, everything drifting gently apart as they went — which is exactly what the real universe does, and exactly why measuring it is a nightmare.

And it really was a nightmare. Nadia tried to pace it, the way she'd paced the tree — and by the time she'd taken ten steps the ground had quietly grown under her, and the ten steps didn't mean what they'd meant when she started. She tried again from a standstill and got a different number again, because the road itself had stretched in between. Measure a thing that's growing *while* you measure it, and your answer depends on *when* you looked, and *from where*, and how fast it was stretching at the time — which is the whole maddening reason the real cosmos won't hand its astronomers one clean number either. Errol watched her swear at the ground and said, mild as milk — *"aye. Now you know why they can't agree. It isn't them that's wrong. It's the road that won't sit still."*

There were two places you could read its speed from. Far back, at the very beginning of the country, was the **afterglow** — the oldest light, the first warmth, where you could clock how fast things were set moving right at the start. And up at the near end, where the crew were standing, *now*, you could clock the speed off the bright nearby beacons — the exploding stars. So they split up to measure it, and that, it turns out, was the mistake and the lesson both.

Because Errol's lot read it from the afterglow and got one speed. And Nadia's lot read it from the nearby beacons and got a *faster* one. And they came back, and compared, and it was the tree all over again but cosmic — both careful, both certain, both *right*, and the numbers flat refused to match. And here's where it got ugly, because they're family and that's worse: Nadia insisting the near measurement was obviously the true one, Errol insisting you can't trust a thing you haven't measured from the source, voices up, the whole crew taking sides — the little human version of the exact war the gowns have been having for a decade.

And it got personal, the way it only does with people you love. Nadia said Errol's whole method was lazy — *"you sighted it off a ridge, you didn't even walk it, you never walk anything"* — and Errol, who does not raise his voice, raised it half an inch, which from Errol is a roar — *"I've measured more in forty years than you've walked in your life, lass. Don't tell me my eye's wrong."* And the crew split clean down the middle — Sol and Danny drifting to Nadia, Matthew and Frances to Errol — and for a horrible few minutes it wasn't a crew at all, it was two camps with a cold patch of ground between them, each dead certain, each clutching their receipts.

And then they did the worst possible thing, which is the thing everyone does: each camp stormed off to *re-measure*, separately, determined to come back with proof the other was wrong. And of course they didn't. Nadia's lot read the beacons again and got the fast number *again*, and waved it like a flag. Errol's lot read the afterglow again and got the slow number *again*, and folded their arms. Both camps came back not reconciled but *more* dug in, each now armed with a figure they'd checked twice — which is, beat for beat, what a decade of the real Hubble tension actually looks like: two sides, both careful, both re-checking and re-checking and getting their own answer every single time, and the gap between them not closing one inch. The crew had managed to reinvent the entire crisis in an afternoon — and they looked, by the end of it, a bit heartbroken about it, because they're a family, and the gowns who do this for a living somehow are not.

And the Gardener let them go at it for a minute, and then said — *"stop. Stop picking a winner. Look at the ground between the two stations. We keep assuming one of you is wrong because we assume you're measuring the same number. What if you're not?"*

## The step

So they looked at the ground between the afterglow and the now. And there was a **step** in it. A clean ledge they'd all been walking over without clocking, between the early road and the late road — and Sol, who sees these things, crouched and put a hand on it and went quiet, and then traced its make — *"it's built. Look. There's a slab here, takes up two-thirds of the height — that's the dark-energy part, the vacuum share, same two-thirds that sits at the still centre. And it's resting on a little tower, three rungs, doubling — two, four, eight. The slab's two-thirds, the tower's eight, and the step is one over the other. The step is a twelfth."*

So they tried it. Errol read his afterglow number one more time; Nadia read her beacons one more time; and this time the Gardener made them put the step — the twelfth — into the gap between the two. And the two numbers that had nearly broken the crew slid together and *sat down side by side*, easy as anything, like they'd never had a cross word in their lives. Nadia stared at it. Errol stared at it. And then Errol said the thing that mends it, low — *"...so we were both right."* And Nadia, grudging, then helpless, then grinning — *"we were both right."* Nobody had won. Which was the only way that fight was ever going to end well, because the answer was never one of them or the other — it was the step underneath, that neither had thought to look for.

A twelfth. That was the whole thing — the early road and the late road were never the same road at two speeds, they were one road with a forced step of a twelfth between the two places you read it. One, plus a twelfth: **thirteen-twelfths**, the offset the country had quietly built into itself out of the dark-energy two-thirds and a three-rung tower. And when they held that thirteen-twelfths up against what the real telescopes see — the early rate and the late rate, and the gap between them — it matched to about one part in a thousand.

So that's the answer to two people and a tree. They can both be right and still not add up — *if there's a step between them.* The universe isn't expanding at two speeds because it's broken. It's expanding at speeds that differ by exactly thirteen-twelfths, because that step is forced, and both measurements are honest, and the "crisis" was everyone assuming two different roads had to give the same number.

Danny — who'd nearly come to blows with Errol twenty minutes earlier and was now sitting propped against him, sharing the warm — put it the way only Danny can. *"So the whole crisis,"* he said, slow, working it out, *"was everyone forgetting to look* down *at the step. For ten years. Billions of quid. All of 'em looking up, none of 'em looking down."* And the Gardener said, *"that's the job, love. They get paid to look up."*

## A crisis is a career

And here's the bit. The crew were *delighted* — a decade-long crisis, dissolved into a tidy little step you could put your hand on. Team Tenure were *devastated.* Because — and this is the quiet part, again — the Dean didn't want the tension *resolved.* A crisis is a career. *"Broken physics"* is a funding stream that runs for thirty years. The Dean went grey and insisted that *"it is far too soon to rule out a fundamental crisis,"* which is a sentence that means *please, please let it still be broken.* Rupert drafted *"Concerns Regarding A Suspiciously Tidy Answer."* And up the matter went to Consensus, naturally, because a crisis this profitable can't be called off without the boss's say-so — and the boss, true to form, declined to say anything that could ever be checked, leaving every one of them free to keep the wound open and the grants flowing. So off they blasted in a thundercloud of grievance, to go and have the crisis somewhere it could still be billed for.

And Priya — ignored, as ever — had quietly checked thirteen-twelfths against the two real measured rates while the men argued, and found it sound to a part in a thousand. She didn't say anything. But she'd written it down. And this time, when the Dean swept past, she didn't hide the paper. She didn't hide it this time. That's how it always starts — with the not-hiding.

## Someone laid the step

And walking home across the gently growing country, the crew were quiet, and nobody noticed — they never do — that the step they'd found had been laid so *carefully*, two-thirds over three rungs, like someone had built it on purpose so the two roads would always meet again if you only thought to look down. Like the whole country had been measured out, long before, by something that wanted it to add up. You can feel it, if you go still. Patient. Fond. Good at sums.

…built it on *purpose*, did I say? Careful, now, you'll have me giving the game away. Somebody laid that step, mind — squared it, levelled it, set it just so a thousand years before there was anyone to trip over it. And the *who* of that is the best part of the whole story. Which is exactly why I'm saving it. Off to bed.
