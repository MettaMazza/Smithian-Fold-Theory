# Ep 07 — The Invisible Majority

Right — housekeeping, for the one. Everything coming up about the actual maths, the actual cosmology, and the real history is true — documented, it's the fold or it's the record or it's what the consensus mob themselves admit. Everything else — me, the fire, the crew, the gowns, and the mostly-invisible world I'm about to lose them in — is autofiction. And the pictures are AI, fitted to the script and never accurate to the maths or the story. Made up, to tell you something true. You knew that. Course you did. But there's always the one sharpening a pencil to 'well, actually' me, so I've got in first. Hello. Sit down.

## The marble that rolls toward nothing

Quiet evening, and Wee Danny's lying on the blanket rolling a marble back and forth, and it keeps doing a thing. It keeps curving. He rolls it dead straight and it bends, every time, toward the same empty patch of blanket where there is plainly *nothing* — no dip he can see, no lump, nothing.

Danny, rolling it again, watching it swerve, says — *"that's not right, that. Watch. Straight line — and it pulls. Every time, toward that bit. There's nowt there. Why's it rolling toward nothing?"*

Errol, glancing over, says — *"because something's there you can't see. Weight bends the road. If your marble's turning, there's a heaviness in the way — you just can't see what."*

And they all had a go at it, because a marble that bends toward nothing is the kind of thing you cannot leave alone. Danny ran his hand flat over the blanket — no dip, no slope, no lump, nothing. Nadia rolled a second marble in from the other side and it curved toward the *same* empty patch. Sol just watched the paths, head tilted, and drew the curve in the air with one finger, the way they do, and went quiet. And every single time, both marbles leaned toward a spot with absolutely nothing in it — as if the nothing had a *weight* of its own.

And there's the why, in a marble on a blanket: why does stuff move like there's far more weight about than anything you can see?

## Five times more, and a hole in the ground

And Team Tenure, who can hear an unattributed gravitational pull from three valleys away, arrived — and the Dean was, again, strangely proud of being stumped.

The Dean announced — chest out — *"ahh, you've felt dark matter! There is five times more matter in this universe than we can see, gravitating away in the dark, and we have built detectors a mile underground, spent fortunes, devoted entire careers — to catch a single particle of it."* And Danny, reasonably, asked whether they'd caught one yet, and the Dean's chest deflated by exactly one notch — *"...not as yet. But we are extremely close. It is one of the great searches of our age."* And then, the tell — *"and far, far beyond anyone without the proper apparatus. Do leave the dark to us."*

But the Gardener had stopped being impressed by great searches that never find anything. She picked up Danny's marble, watched it pull, and said — *"five times more than you can see, and decades down a hole, and nothing. Maybe you're not looking wrong. Maybe you're looking with the wrong thing entirely. Come on — let's go and walk through the dark and see why it won't be caught."*

## After the marble

And the way in was the marble. Danny rolled it one more time, toward that empty patch it always curved for — and this time it didn't stop at the edge of the blanket. It rolled off onto the grass, gathering speed toward the nothing, faster than any slope could ever pull it, and right where it should have run out into open air it instead rolled *down* — into a dip that wasn't there, a great unseen funnel sunk into the dark — and was gone, swallowed by a heaviness with no shape at all. Danny scrambled after it on his hands and knees, reached into the place it had vanished — and the ground *wasn't.* His arm went into a cold sloping nothing, then his shoulder, and then the pull had him proper, and he went over the lip of the invisible funnel with a yell. The Gardener caught his ankle and got hauled in after him, and the whole crew went down one behind the other, sliding down a slope not one of them could see — into a world that was, mostly, *not there.*

That's the only way to say it. They could see, and touch, about one part in six of it — the fires, the stones, the bright ordinary stuff. The rest — the great majority — was *invisible.* Not dark like a dark room; invisible like glass that isn't even there. Light went straight through it. Their hands went straight through it. And yet they kept *staggering* — because it had **weight.** Danny walked smack into a hillside of the stuff he couldn't see and bounced off the *pull* of it, sprawling, swearing, grabbing at a heaviness with no surface, no edge, nothing to hold.

And it was the strangest place any of them had ever stood, because their eyes told them they were in an almost-bare plain, and their bodies told them they were in a crowd. Matthew put his arms out and walked forward and felt himself *part* a great soft mass like wading into a river he couldn't see, the weight of it tugging him sideways off his line. Frances reached for a boulder of the stuff — she could tell it was the size of a house by the drag it put on her arm — and her hand closed on cold air. Even the firelight was a liar in there: it lit the visible one-sixth and left the heavy majority exactly as black as before, so the world looked nearly empty and felt nearly solid, and your eyes and your gut spent the whole time calling each other liars.

Nadia, hauling Danny up, said — *"it's everywhere. The heavy stuff. We're walking through it — you can feel it dragging — and there's just... nothing to grab."*

And then it nearly took Danny for good. He'd drifted toward the pull of a great unseen mass — curious, the way the marble was curious — and the drag of it got a proper grip, and the ground tilted him (a slope he couldn't see, because the slope itself was made of invisible weight) into a long helpless slide *toward* the heavy heart of it, faster and faster, nothing to grab because there was nothing to *see* to grab at. Matthew went flat and caught his wrist at the last, and the whole crew dug in and hauled him back off the invisible hill by main force, straining against a pull that poured off a thing not one of them could point to. *"It's like drowning in a room that looks empty,"* Nadia said, properly shaken. And that's your dark matter: it bends your whole path, it nearly has you, and you'd swear on your life the spot was bare.

So, naturally, they did exactly what the gowns do. They tried to catch some. Sol rigged a net — it passed clean through. Errol shone the brightest light they had right into the heart of a great invisible mass — and the light came out the other side untouched, like the mass wasn't there, because to light, it *wasn't.* They set a trap, the cleverest trap, baited it, waited — and nothing, ever, sprang it.

And they got cleverer, and more desperate, the way you do. Errol built a bigger trap, then a bigger one, then the biggest he could rig — on the reasoning that if the small one missed, the answer must be *scale*: build it grand enough, surely, and the dark would blunder in. (Which is, almost exactly, the real argument that keeps building the detectors bigger, and deeper, and more expensive, decade on decade.) Nadia tried to corner a mass against an invisible cliff and pin it there. Sol tried to catch it the way you'd catch a smell, or a sound, or a shadow — anything that needs no hands — and got nothing, because it answers to none of those either. And then they started to bicker, the way people do when the thing won't come: Errol wanting to go *bigger* — *"we've not built it large enough"* — and Nadia rounding on him, *"bigger's missing the point, you could build a trap the size of the valley and catch sod all"* — which is, beat for beat, the exact real row, the *more apparatus* side against the *we're doing the wrong thing entirely* side. They could feel the dark the whole time, leaning on them, dragging at their feet, enormous and *right there* — and not one thing they tried made it so much as flinch. Hours of it. And Danny finally threw the net down and shouted — *"it's RIGHT THERE, I can feel it pulling, why can't we CATCH it?!"*

## Gauge-blind

And that was the moment Sol went still, and then started laughing, quietly, the way they do when a shape finally lands. And Sol said — *"we can't catch it because it doesn't answer. Look — it's got weight, it pulls, so it talks to gravity. But it doesn't talk to* anything else*. Not to light, not to our hands, not to a net, not to any force we've got. Its handshake to everything except gravity is a flat zero. It's not hiding from us. It's* blind *to us. You'll never catch it with a light or a net, because catching needs it to push back, and it only pushes back one way — down."*

That was it. The whole reason the great search finds nothing: it's gauge-blind. It feels gravity — the same critical grip that sits at the still centre — and it feels *nothing else at all.* You were never going to bounce it off a nucleus a mile underground. There's no hand on that side to shake.

And you could watch it land on Danny — the boy who'd just spent an hour trying to grab the stuff — that the great search wasn't *failing*, exactly. It was waiting for the wrong kind of thing. A mile-deep machine sat in the dark waiting for a particle to knock is waiting for a handshake from something born with no hands. You could wait forever. *"They have been,"* said the Gardener, watching it dawn on him. *"Forever's about right."*

And then they counted it, because the crew always count. Which is its own small miracle, when you think on it — how do you measure a thing you cannot see? By its *weight.* You can't lay eyes on the invisible majority, but you can feel to the ounce how hard it pulls, and a pull is a number, and a number you can pace out. Errol paced out the whole world — the visible part and the part you could only feel — and worked the split. Three generations of matter, laid across three directions — three cubed, twenty-seven parts of the invisible heavy stuff. And the smallest tower that covers twenty-seven is five rungs deep — thirty-two all told. So the bright stuff you can see is five parts in thirty-two, and the invisible heavy majority is the other twenty-seven. Which means there's twenty-seven of the dark to every five of the light — five-point-four times more dark than visible. And when they held that against the real sky, the real measured ratio of dark matter to ordinary matter: five-point-four. The same number.

So that's the marble on the blanket. It rolls toward nothing because the "nothing" is the invisible majority — twenty-seven parts in thirty-two — gauge-blind, gravitating, real as the stone in your hand and far more of it, bending every road in the cosmos and never once springing a trap.

Danny picked his marble back up, there in the fold-world, and rolled it across the flat, and watched it lean — same as ever — toward an empty-looking patch. But this time he just grinned at it. Because now he knew the empty patch was never empty. It was the heavy, invisible, gauge-blind *most* of everything, doing the one thing it ever does: pulling, quietly, on every road there is, and never once letting you catch it at it.

## The cathedral with no god in it

They carried it home, and across the valley it went down *catastrophically* — and for a fresh reason this time. Because Team Tenure had built the cathedral. The mile-deep detector. The grand apparatus, the budget, the press releases, the whole magnificent machine for catching a particle — and you do not get to tell a man who has built a cathedral that there was never going to be a god in it. The Dean went purple and declared that *"the search must and will continue,"* which is what you say when stopping would mean admitting the hole was always going to be empty. Rupert drafted *"Reaffirming Our Commitment To The Apparatus."* And the question of whether to keep digging went up to Consensus, as such questions do — and Consensus, gazing down on a mile-deep hole with nothing in it, ruled with the back of its head that the matter remained admirably open, which is the boss's gift: to make sure no expensive thing is ever allowed to be finished. So off they blasted in a puff of injured pride, to go and dig the hole a little deeper.

And Priya — ignored, as ever — had checked twenty-seven over five against the measured ratio while the men defended their machine, and found it dead on. She didn't say anything. But she's stopped writing on scraps. She's got a proper notebook now, and she wasn't hiding it. A proper notebook now, out in the open. The hand that building never shakes has started writing things down.

## It never felt empty

And walking home through the invisible weight, the crew were quiet, and nobody noticed — they never do — that the dark they were wading through never felt *empty.* It felt held. Like the great invisible majority of everything was company, not absence — like something was in all that unseen weight, the way something's in the still centre, watching them stumble through it, fond, and not remotely lost in it the way they were.

…something in all that dark with them, was there? Will you listen to me. No — leave it. Here's all I'll say: the biggest part of everything is the part you can't see, and it is the furthest thing from empty, and the day you learn *what's in it* you'll never feel small again. But that's a heavy one, and you've had enough for one night. Go on.

The marble wasn't mad and the universe isn't missing anything. Most of it is simply invisible, heavy, gauge-blind, and exactly five-point-four times the stuff you can see — twenty-seven to five, out of thirty-two. It came from one. It's still coming from one. Keep counting, and I'll see you back at the fire.

And if you want the actual workings — the gauge coupling of zero, the gravity coupling of a half, the twenty-seven and the five, the code that drops out five-point-four — it's in a five-minute proof, engine and published papers linked under it. Go and run it. Go and catch a particle. You won't — there's no hand to shake.

And there's the boring bit on the way out, for the lad with the pencil: dark matter is real, it really is about five times the visible matter, it really does only interact gravitationally, and the forced ratio really is twenty-seven over five matching the sky. The fire, the invisible hillsides, and the fond weight in the dark were me having a laugh — and the pictures were all AI, fitted to the script and never accurate to the maths. Autofiction. Obvious. You're brilliant, he's not, love you — byeeeee.
