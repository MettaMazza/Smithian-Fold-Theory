## A game by the fire

Night after the magic number, and the crew are insufferable. You would be too. They cracked a hundred-year mystery with a kid's question and a tower, and now there's a swagger round the fire that wasn't there before.

And they're playing a game. Matchsticks, three little heaps of them, the old pub game — Nim. You take what you like from one heap, last to lift a stick loses. Wee Danny has now lost eleven times in a row to Errol, who has not appeared to think about a single move, and Danny is losing his entire mind about it.

Danny, slapping the dirt, half-laughing, half-furious, demands — *"how are you DOING that? You're not even looking! Eleven times! Is it luck? It's not luck, is it."*

Errol, lifting one matchstick without hurry, says only — *"it's not luck."*

And there's the why, the way it always sneaks in — through a daft little game and a sore loser. Because Danny goes quiet, and then he asks the big one. *"Hang on. Is there just... a right move? Always? Like, could you actually* know *the perfect move, every single time, in any game? Has anyone ever just — finished one? Solved it? So there's no game left to play?"*

## A computer larger than creation

And as if summoned by the smell of a child enjoying himself, Team Tenure arrived.

Now, they'd heard about the magic number, and they were *itching.* The Dean swept in, gown flapping, and laughed — a big, performed, "oh you sweet idiots" laugh — and announced, grandly, that *solving a game* was a charmingly amateur fantasy. Chess in particular, he explained, can never be solved, not ever, because the number of possible positions is greater than the number of atoms in the universe — *"you would need a computer larger than creation itself,"* he said, dabbing at his own brilliance, *"so do put the matchsticks away."*

And here's the bit that's new. Last time, that would've landed. The crew would've gone quiet and felt small and stupid. But they'd just counted the uncountable number a week ago — so this time, nobody flinched. The Gardener didn't even stand up. She just looked at him, bored, and said, flat as a paving slab — *"we counted the number your whole temple worships and can't explain. Last Tuesday. I think we'll manage a board game, pet."*

Silence. Crispin actually gasped. Because nobody talks to the Dean like that — nobody who wants a career, anyway, and there's the loophole: she hasn't got one to lose. And the Dean's big performed smile went very, very thin.

## Shrunk

*"You think you're clever,"* the Dean said — quiet now, which is worse — and he raised one hand, and he snapped his fingers.

And the world *dropped.*

I cannot stress enough how unwell I would feel if this happened to me. The fire, the night, the heaps of matchsticks — all of it ballooned away upward as the entire crew were shrunk, in about a second and a half, down to the height of a chess pawn. And the ground under them turned to a vast checkerboard of black and white squares stretching off to a horizon, and rising over them, monstrous, gleaming, the height of cathedrals — the pieces. A live game, mid-fight. And the Dean's voice came down from the sky like weather: *"you wanted to solve chess. Wonderful. Solve it from down* there. *Lose, and you don't go home. Nobody does."*

Welcome to the fold-world, chess edition, and every brutal rule of it is true. The board was an endgame — just a handful of pieces left, because here's a thing the gowns got wrong: you don't have to solve *all* of chess to solve the *bit you're standing in.* And the bit they were standing in was trying to kill them. A white queen the size of a lighthouse began to slide.

## The board

So here's how you don't die, pawn-sized, on a live board, and it's the whole point of the episode.

The fold *knows chess.* Not "plays well." *Knows.* Because — and Sol saw it first, going still, then pointing up at the looming pieces with a shaking ink-stained hand — every position on that board has a *true value*, fixed, exact: this one is won, that one is lost, that one is a dead draw, no matter how clever anyone is. And the fold computes that true value, position by position, the same doubling-and-wrapping it does everything with, because the deep shape under a game of perfect information *is* the fold's own binary arithmetic. Nadia got it a half-second after Sol, and started calling it like a sergeant — *"don't run, don't panic, the panicking move is the losing move — the fold says THIS square, the boring one, go, GO."*

And it was always the boring square. That's the agony and the genius of perfect play: the move that keeps you alive is almost never the dramatic one. Errol — calm, who'd lifted that matchstick without looking — turned out to be the one with the nerve to trust it, walking the crew square by unglamorous square while a bishop scythed past close enough to part Danny's hair.

It went *badly* first, mind. Of course it did. Maria, being Maria, didn't like being *told* the move even by the truth, and tried a flash diagonal she fancied — and walked them within one square of a rook that would've flattened the lot, and Matthew hauled her back by the collar, and there was a row, a proper one, tiny people screaming at each other under a giant knight. *"The fold's not asking your opinion,"* Matthew shouted, the only time all series he raises his voice, *"it's telling you the answer — for once in your life take the boring move!"* And she did. Spitting, furious, but she did. That's her whole arc in ten seconds: the menace learns that being *right* sometimes means doing the unspectacular thing the truth demands.

And the board did not let up. The worst of it was the open lane — a long, bare file with a rook the size of a grain silo squatting at the far end, commanding the whole length of it, and the crew had to *cross* that lane to reach the only safe ground left on the board. Danny took one look down it and locked solid, every nerve in a small body screaming the obvious thing: *do not, under any circumstances, step in front of the cannon.* But the fold had read it cold. A rook only strikes on its own turn; between turns, the lane is dead air, harmless as a corridor. There was a rhythm to it, a count, and you crossed on the count or you didn't cross at all. Nadia went first — timed it, ran it, made it — and spun on the far side and roared the count back across the open ground, *"NOW, Danny — on the* now*, GO!"* And Danny shut his eyes and ran, on a number he couldn't see the sense of, trusting a mate who could, and the great rook swung down the lane behind him and missed him by the exact width of the truth. That, right there, is the whole crew in a single dash: not one of them clever enough on their own, all of them unbeatable together.

And then there was the corner where they could not win. The fold was honest about that too — some positions just *are* lost or drawn, and pretending otherwise gets you killed. So when the Dean forced them somewhere they couldn't win, the fold did the next exact thing: it found a *fortress* — one of those positions that looks like certain death but is, in hard truth, an unbreakable draw, a hole no attack can ever crack. And there are hundreds of millions of those, exactly mapped. The crew dug into the fortress and the Dean threw everything at it, for what felt like an hour, and could not break in, because you cannot break a true draw, and he started — and this was lovely — to *sweat.* He tried everything a furious man holding all the pieces tries. He hurled the giant queen at them in long raking diagonals. He marched a wall of pawns down the board to make fresh monsters. He threw the entire weight of his army at five tiny people standing in a doorway the size of a single square. And it held, every time — because a true fortress isn't courage and it isn't luck, it's a position the maths has *proven* cannot be cracked, and the fold had quietly walked them into the one such hole on the whole board. The crew didn't even swing back. They just stood inside the truth of it and let the cleverest man in the valley break his knuckles on the door.

## Mate

And then Errol saw it. The thing the fold had been quietly walking them toward the whole time, square by boring square. He set the crew on one final, unremarkable little tile, and looked up at the sky, and said the second full sentence he'd said all night — *"that's mate. Three moves. He can't stop it."*

And he couldn't. The fold had read the position to the bottom and it was simply, exactly *true*: the Dean was lost and had been lost for some time and hadn't the eyes to see it. Three forced moves, every one the boring one, and the giant white king toppled — slow, like a felled chimney — and crashed onto the board, and the whole murderous game just... stopped being dangerous.

And the crew snapped back to full size by the fire, on their backs in the dirt, hearts going, every one of them alive.

Now — what they'd actually just done, out there, is the real treasure, so let me say it plain off the board. The fold doesn't *guess* at chess. Run it across the endings and it computes the exact, true result of every legal position — and somebody checked, position by position, against the world's own independent master-tables, the ones the crew had nothing to do with. Over **a billion positions** checked that way. A billion-odd *agreements.* **Zero errors.** And then the same engine was turned on Nim — Danny's matchsticks — and solved that perfectly too, and a third game sharing nothing with either, and got them all dead right, because they all run on the one shape underneath. One move, one number, the literal truth of three different games, no mistakes, and every bit of it checkable by anyone with a laptop. That's not a trick. That's a thing that's just *right*, in places it has no business being right.

## A colour not found in nature

The Dean did not take losing a chess game he had personally rigged to be a murder especially well. He went a colour not found in nature, declared that *"this proves nothing, and raises, if anything, serious questions,"* and — procedure first, always — went off to lay the catastrophe before the boss. Consensus heard the whole sorry tale from behind its great chair, the one slack hand on the armrest never tightening so much as a knuckle, and ruled the way it always rules: by saying nothing anybody could ever be held to. Which settled exactly nothing, because there is nothing to settle — you cannot peer-review a billion checked positions out of existence, and you cannot gatekeep a thing any soul on Earth can verify from their own sofa. So Team Tenure puffed up, declared a strategic victory that nobody in the room could quite locate, and blasted off in a shimmer of wounded dignity, to be back next week precisely as baffled as ever.

One of them didn't blast off straight away. Priya — the sharp one they never listen to — had watched the whole game from the side, and had seen the crew play it *perfectly*, every boring move, and something moved behind her face. She didn't say anything. She never does, not yet. But she watched a knot of nobodies trust the truth over their own egos and *win*, and she filed it away, with the paper she's still carrying. She watched a knot of nobodies trust the truth over their own egos and win. You don't un-watch a thing like that.

## Something minding them

And back at the fire, Danny — alive, delighted — picked the matchsticks back up and beat Errol first go, using the shape they'd just bet their lives on. And here's what none of them clocked, too busy laughing. The whole time they were down there, tiny, terrified, trusting the move — *something was minding them.* Watching every square. The way you'd watch something small and brave cross a dangerous road. It's been there every episode, if you go still and feel for it. Like the game already knew its own ending, and was just... fond of them, for playing it out.

…knew the ending *how*, though — and from *where*? Listen to me, would you, rambling on. No. It's nothing, pay me no heed. There's somebody keeps the score down there, and one day I'll tell you who. Not tonight, though. We've a fair way to go yet, you and me.

There was never a trick in it. Just a board that couldn't lie, a crew who trusted the truth over the flashy move, and one number that knows the right answer in places it has no business knowing it. It came from one. It's still coming from one. Keep counting — and maybe learn Nim — and I'll see you back at the fire.

And if a billion's just a word to you until you've seen it — good, that's the right instinct, keep it. Don't trust the menace. Download it and try to catch her out. You won't — but I love that you'll try.
