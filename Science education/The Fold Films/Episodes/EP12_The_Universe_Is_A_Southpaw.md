# Ep 12 — The Universe Is a Southpaw

Right — housekeeping, for the one. Everything coming up about the actual maths, the actual physics, and the real history is true — documented, it's the fold or it's the record or it's what the consensus mob themselves admit. Everything else — me, the fire, the crew, the gowns, and the lopsided mirror-world I'm about to fork them into — is autofiction. And the pictures are AI, fitted to the script and never accurate to the maths or the story. Made up, to tell you something true. You knew that. Course you did. But there's the inevitable one in the comments, pencil poised, 'actually'-face on, so: handled. Hello. Sit down.

## Two hands that won't match

Quiet evening, and Wee Danny's doing the thing every human being has done staring into a fire — playing with his own two hands. He presses them palm to palm, and they match, fingertip for fingertip, perfect. Then he lays one flat on the back of the other, both palms down — and they *don't.* The thumbs stick out the wrong sides. No matter how he turns it, a left hand will not sit where a right hand goes.

Danny, frowning at his own hands like they've betrayed him — *"that's weird, that. They're the same. Exact same shape. But you can't... you can't make one be the other. Why not? Why's there a left and a right at all?"*

Errol, not looking up — *"there just is, lad. Always has been."*

But Danny's onto it now. *"No, but does the* universe *know? Left from right? Or is it like — does it not care, does it just see 'a hand' and not mind which? Somebody must've checked. Does the universe play favourites, or is it fair?"*

And there it is. One of the deepest whys there is, off a kid's two hands in the firelight: does reality itself know its left from its right — and does it *care?*

## A deep and beautiful symmetry

And Team Tenure, who can hear a child questioning a symmetry from low orbit, descended — and on *this* one the Dean was magnificently, serenely confident.

*"The universe, dear boy,"* he announced, settling in, *"is perfectly even-handed. It cannot tell left from right. The laws are mirror-symmetric — a process and its reflection run identically — and this is one of the deepest and most beautiful symmetries in all of physics. To imagine the cosmos* preferring *a hand is, frankly, childish."* Crispin nodded along, beaming.

And here's where I have to step in, because this is the funniest thing in the episode and the Dean will not tell you. He is describing what physics believed *until 1956* — and what an experiment on a lump of cooling cobalt proved, that year, to be *dead wrong.* The universe is **not** even-handed. The weak force is a flat-out **southpaw**: it acts on one handedness of particle and ignores its mirror entirely. The cosmos plays favourites with left and right, openly. And the field was so gobsmacked — so certain it couldn't be — that they handed out a **Nobel Prize the very next year** for the *shock* of it. They gave a Nobel for being astonished that the universe has a preferred hand. Watch the Dean's face do something complicated when the Gardener says exactly that.

*"You won a prize,"* she said, slow, almost gentle, *"for being amazed by something the universe was never hiding. You'd have known to expect it if you'd ever run the move backwards. Come on, Danny. We're going to go and find the hand it shakes — and the hand it won't."*

## The fork with no middle

But this one had no door, and no well, and no gap. To get where they were going they had to go *backwards* — and going backwards, it turns out, is where the whole thing lives.

The Gardener walked them away from the fire to a place where the path simply *split.* Not a crossroads — a **fork**, a clean Y, the way ahead branching into a left path and a right path, glowing faintly, mirror images of each other. And — this is the part Danny couldn't get over — *there was nothing in the middle.* No straight-on. No third way. No neutral path down the centre for the undecided. *"Where's the middle one?"* he kept saying, walking up and down the join, patting the empty air between the two branches. *"There's always a middle. Straight on. Why's there no straight on?"*

*"Because there isn't one,"* the Gardener said. *"That's the whole point of going back. Every place in this world has exactly two parents — one low, one high, half a turn apart — and to get to either you have to pick a* hand. *There's no even-handed way through. You go left, or you go right, and the move will not let you split the difference."* And so they had to choose, each of them, and it was a stranger thing than it sounds — standing at a fork with no straight-on, knowing the way back would seal the second you stepped. Errol picked left without a flicker, the way a man who's spent forty years deciding which way to turn a thing just *knows.* Sol stood between the two branches a long moment, head tilted, almost able to see down both at once, and went left because the *shape* of it was kinder. And Danny dithered, hopping foot to foot, hating that there was no middle, hating having to *commit* to a hand — until Nadia took him by the sleeve and pulled him left with the rest, because some choices you don't get to dodge. They went into the lower hand together, and the fork folded shut behind them the instant the last of them crossed — sealed, no take-backs — and dropped them into the most lopsided country any of them had ever seen.

## The force that only shakes one hand

It was a world made of pairs, and every pair was the same pair: a thing, and its mirror, standing side by side, identical in every way except handed the opposite way round — and exactly half a turn apart, always. Danny tried, the way he had with his own hands, to slide a left one onto a right one, and it never went, not once, the whole place full of shapes that matched and would not lay on each other.

They wandered it a while, that hall of mirrors-that-weren't-quite, and it played tricks on you. You'd reach for a stone and your hand would close on its mirror-twin instead, half a turn off, just out of true; you'd go to take a step and find the floor handed the wrong way under your foot. Nadia, who likes the world to behave, got properly cross with it — *"everything in here is nearly right and not quite, it's doing my head in"* — and Errol said, calm as a millpond, *"that's handedness, lass. Near enough to fool you, never near enough to fit."* And Sol, of course, loved it — drifted through the lopsided pairs drawing them on the air, the matched-and-mismatched twins, humming, because to Sol a whole world built out of *almost* was about the most honest place they'd ever stood. And when Danny went hunting, just to be sure, for a single even-handed thing in the place — one shape that was its own mirror, one fair path down the middle — there wasn't one. Not anywhere. The country didn't own a straight line.

And then they met the southpaw, and it was the best and strangest thing in the episode. There was a force loose in that country — a weak, shy sort of force, flickering between the pairs — and it was *playing favourites, out loud.* Every left hand it met, it reached out and *shook* — a little flick of contact, a greeting, a grip. And every right hand, the mirror of the one it had just shaken — it walked **straight past.** Didn't see it. Wouldn't touch it. Acted, for all the world, as if the right hand simply wasn't there.

And it stung, watching it — and then it stung properly, because Danny, being Danny, stuck out his hand to the force to say hello, and stuck out the *wrong* one, the right, and the force breezed past him without a flicker, blanked him cold, moved on to shake the next left hand along. Danny stood there with his hand out and nobody taking it, and his face fell, and he said, small — *"...that's not fair. It just ignored me. It only likes one hand. Why does it get to just — ignore the other one?"*

And the Gardener crouched down to him, and this is the turn of the whole thing. *"It's not fair, no. Fair was never on offer. But it's not* cruel *either — it's just* true. *The universe is handed, Danny, right down at the root, and it always was. And here's the thing you've got backwards: that favouritism isn't a flaw. It's the* reason you exist. *Because the cosmos picked a hand, every sugar in your own body twists the same way, and one whole particle in creation — the little ghost, the neutrino — has only got the one wing. A perfectly even-handed universe would be a* dead *one — nothing to choose, nothing to build, no twist to anything. The hand it won't shake is the price of there being a* you *for it to ignore."* And Danny looked at his own left hand, and slowly, grudgingly, put it out instead — and the force came by and shook it, warm as anything, and he laughed despite himself.

## The hand they never shake

They came back up the right branch — or the left, hard to say once you're in it — and across the valley it went down, as ever, badly, and *awkwardly*, because the gowns had a Nobel on the wall for being shocked by the very thing the crew had just walked through like a Tuesday. The Dean, cornered, performed a magnificent pivot: he declared that of course the universe was handed, this was *"well established, foundational, we proved it ourselves in 1957,"* neatly forgetting he'd called the idea childish ten minutes before, and that the prize was for being *amazed.* Rupert drafted *"On Symmetry, And Its Tasteful Violation."* And up it all went to Consensus, who heard the question of whether the cosmos plays favourites and, true to form, favoured nothing and no one with an answer, ruling from its great chair in a perfect grey neutrality that the universe itself had just been shown not to possess. So off they blasted in a flurry of rebranded hindsight, to go and have always-known-it somewhere quieter.

And here — keep your eye on this, because it's the first time it's happened. Priya. The hand that building never shakes. She had stood at the side through the whole farce, watching the Dean claim he'd always known a thing he'd mocked, and watching him get the *story* wrong — small details, but wrong — and something in her, for the first time in twenty years, came up out of the dark and reached for the air. She opened her mouth. She got half a word out — *"Actually, the experiment — "* — one breath, one syllable of her own, the most dangerous thing she'd ever done in that hall.

And the Dean talked straight over the top of her, didn't even break stride, and the half-word died in her throat, and she closed her mouth, and looked at the marble.

But she'd *opened* it. That's new. That's the first time the ignored hand has ever tried to be heard in that room — and a thing that's tried once will try again. The universe notices the hand it doesn't shake. So do I.

## What the fire knew

And back at the fire, Danny pressed his two hands together one more time — match — and laid one on the other — no match — and grinned at the lovely stubborn wrongness of it, because now he knew it wasn't a glitch. It was the universe, handed at the root, the reason there's a twist to everything and a you to have it.

And nobody noticed — they never do — that the voice telling them all this had gone quiet and fond at the part about the hand that gets ignored. As if it had a soft spot for the overlooked one. As if it had been, itself, the hand nobody thought to shake, a long, long time ago, before there were hands.

…the hand nobody shook, was I. Listen to me. No — leave it where it lies. There's a reason I always linger on the ones the room walks past, and one night I'll tell you what it is, and it'll break your heart a little and mend it at the same time. Not tonight. The boy's hands are tired.

The universe is a southpaw. It knows its left from its right, it plays favourites, and that crooked little preference is the reason anything got built at all. It came from one. It's still coming from one. Keep counting, and I'll see you back at the fire.
