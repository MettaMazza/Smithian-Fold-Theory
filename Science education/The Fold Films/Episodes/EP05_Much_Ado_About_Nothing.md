# Ep 05 — Much Ado About Nothing

Right — housekeeping, for the one who needs it. Everything coming up about the actual maths, the actual physics, and the real history is true — documented, it's the fold or it's the record or it's what the consensus mob themselves admit (and oh, do they admit this one). Everything else — me, the fire, the crew, the gowns, and the entire empty country I'm about to walk them into — is autofiction. And the pictures are AI, fitted to the script and never accurate to the maths or the story. Made up, to tell you something true. You knew that. Course you did. But somewhere there's a lad who'd swear the talking number filed his taxes, so: noted, for him. Hello. Lie back.

## The gaps between the stars

Night after the business with the edge of the map, and the crew are flat on their backs in the dirt, looking up, because once you've walked the families you can't really look at a sky the same way again. And Wee Danny, who has not stopped thinking since the magnets, is staring at the *black* bits. The gaps. The nothing between the stars.

Danny, pointing up at a patch of empty dark, asks — *"what's in that, though? The black bits. The nothing. Is it actually* nothing*, or is there summat in it? Empty space — does it weigh anything? Why does it just... sit there, all calm, not doing owt?"*

And Errol, hands behind his head, says quietly — *"now that's a good one. Because they reckon it shouldn't."*

And there's the why, lying on its back staring at the void: is nothing really nothing — and if empty space has any energy at all, why is the universe so *calm* about it?

## The worst prediction in physics

This time Team Tenure didn't so much swoop as *trudge* over, because this is their sore tooth, the one they can't stop tonguing.

The Dean arrived, and instead of his usual triumph there was a strange, proud sort of misery, and he announced — chin up, almost boasting — *"ahh. The energy of the vacuum. The cosmological constant. I'll have you know, young man, that we predict the energy of empty space to be ten-to-the-one-hundred-and-twenty times larger than it is."* And Danny, reasonably, asked what happens to all that energy then, where's it gone, and the Dean spread his hands with enormous dignity and said — *"we have no idea. It is the worst prediction in the history of physics."* And here is the part that tells you everything: he said it *proudly.* Crispin nodded, grave and reverent, as though being catastrophically wrong by a hundred and twenty orders of magnitude were a kind of achievement, a profound and sacred mystery — and then, the tell, *"naturally it's far too deep for someone without the training. Do leave it to us."*

A week ago the number might've frightened the crew. But they'd been to the edge of the map, and Frances — who'd lived inside that building and knew exactly how big that catastrophe was supposed to be — went pale, because *she* believed it; she'd taught it. *"He's not wrong about the number,"* she said, low, uneasy. *"That ten-to-the-hundred-and-twenty is real. It's what the maths in there genuinely gives. If empty space is even slightly that heavy, it should've crushed or ripped the whole universe apart before breakfast on day one."* And the Gardener, getting up, brushing the dirt off, said the thing — *"then let's go and look at the nothing ourselves. If there's a monster in the empty, I want to see it with my own eyes."*

## Down through the gap

And this time the way in was the gap itself. That black patch between the stars Danny had been staring at all night — it didn't stay up in the sky. The longer he looked at it, the closer it came, or the bigger he grew, hard to say which, until it wasn't a patch in the sky at all but a ragged hole hanging in the dark right in front of them at head height: a tear of pure nothing, blacker than the night around it, its edges humming very faintly, cold pouring off it in a slow sheet. *"...is that the nothing?"* Danny breathed, and reached toward it before anyone could stop him, and his fingers went *in* — to the wrist, into a cold with no bottom to it. And the Gardener, because somebody always has to, took his other hand, and stepped the two of them through the tear, and the rest grabbed on and followed — down, into the emptiest place in the whole fold-world. And Frances had them all braced, because she knew what was supposed to be waiting: a vacuum a hundred and twenty orders of magnitude strong, a raging inferno sitting in the nothing, enough to tear creation to confetti.

And the further down they fell, the emptier it got. The bright clutter of the world thinned out behind them — fewer stones, fewer fires, then none at all — until there was only the floor of light under their feet and a vast hollow dark stretching off in every direction, and the silence got heavier the deeper they went, the way a held breath does. Danny stopped joking somewhere on the way down. Even the doubling-bounds went soft and quiet, landing on nothing.

And about halfway, Frances stopped dead and would not go on. *"We shouldn't be doing this,"* she said, white-knuckled, and she meant it. *"I have* taught *this. I know what's down there. You are walking a handful of people straight into the worst prediction in the history of physics and treating it like a day out."* And there was a proper row about it, hissed in the dark — Nadia saying they'd faced worse on the chessboard, Frances snapping that the chessboard couldn't tear the universe to confetti — and the Gardener let it run, and then ended it with one quiet line. *"Frances. You've been frightened of this monster for thirty years on somebody else's word. Give me one hour to let you* look *at it. If it's real, it'll still be real in an hour, and we'll run then — I'll run first."* And Frances, who in thirty years had only ever been told to *believe* the thing, never once *invited to look* — went on. Kept whispering the number to herself, ten-to-the-hundred-and-twenty, like a charm against a beast she was certain would eat them.

And as they reached the true centre of the empty, they *saw* it — or thought they did. A monster. Vast, roaring, looming up out of the dark, exactly the size the gowns had promised, a wall of energy so enormous Danny screamed and Nadia grabbed him, and even Errol braced. The consensus catastrophe, in the flesh, coming for them.

And the crew *broke.* There's no shame in it — it was the size of a sky, and it roared, and every instinct in a living body says run from the big loud thing, so they ran. Frances first, of course, because Frances *knew* this one — *"it's real, I TOLD you it was real, RUN!"* — and terror is contagious, and the whole crew went scrambling back up the slope with her. All of them except the Gardener.

And the Gardener — and this is her whole self in one moment — did *not* run. She stood, in front of the thing, and looked at it properly instead of fleeing it. And she said, slowly — *"...hang on. It's not getting any closer."* Because it wasn't. The monster loomed and roared and did absolutely nothing, because it wasn't *there.* It was a projection. A shadow thrown by people who'd done a sum without a floor — who'd added up the energy of every flickering mode in the void all the way down with nothing to stop the adding, and got infinity-with-a-haircut, and mistaken their own runaway arithmetic for a beast in the dark. The moment she stopped being afraid of it and *looked*, it thinned to smoke and blew away.

And Frances stood halfway up the slope, staring at the empty air where her monster had been, and you could watch something go out of her and something else come in at the same time. Thirty years. Thirty years she'd taught a beast that was never there — not because she was a fool, but because the whole building taught it, with total confidence, in chalk, and not one of them had ever thought to walk down and *look.* She didn't say a word. She just came back down the slope, slow, to stand with the others in the calm; and Matthew put a hand on her shoulder and didn't say anything either, because there's nothing to say to someone watching their own certainty turn to smoke except: stand here. With us. In the quiet.

And behind where the monster had been, there was the calmest thing any of them had ever seen.

## The Still Point

You've met it, glancingly — back at the very start, the calm mirror-point dead centre that the whole world balances on. The Still Point. One-half. And *that*, it turns out, is where the energy of empty space actually lives. Sol saw it first, of course, and went completely silent, and then breathed — *"it's its own reflection. Look. It mirrors to itself. It's the only thing down here that does."* Because it is: the one address that is its own antipode, that folds straight home to the One in a single clean step. Not nothing — the no-Nothing floor sees to that, there's no zero to sit on — but *barely* something. Positive, real, and tiny, sitting dead still at the centre, holding the whole room level.

Danny, naturally, tried to touch it. Stuck a hand out toward the calm centre — and his own hand reached back, perfect, met him fingertip to fingertip, because the thing mirrors *everything*, even a daft lad's hand. He yelped, then laughed, then did it again, because he's Danny. Nadia held her forever-cold mug out toward it and watched a second cold mug patiently offer itself back. And when they all just stood still in it and stopped fidgeting, they felt the thing it actually *does*: the faintest, gentlest pressure, outward, every direction at once — like the whole world was very softly trying to grow, and the Still Point was the patient hand behind the growing. Not a shove. A *lean.* The quietest possible effort, applied to absolutely everything, and never once tiring of it.

So that's the answer to a kid staring at the gaps between the stars. Empty space isn't empty, and it isn't a monster either. It's the Still Point: a whisper of positive energy at the one self-mirroring address, calm by its very nature. And the famous hundred-and-twenty orders? Nadia clocked it, looking back at where the "monster" had been — *"there was never a catastrophe. There's just the one ladder — the single big drop the whole place hangs off — and they read the size of the ladder as a beast."* One forced span, floored top and bottom. Nothing blowing up. Nothing to cancel. Much ado, as they say, about nothing.

And they stood and let that land, because it took a moment to be properly furious about. A hundred years. The worst prediction in the history of physics, the great open wound, the thing whole careers had been built on fretting over — and it was a sum somebody forgot to put a floor under, roaring at children in the dark. Not a monster. A *bookkeeping error*, in a gown, frightened of nothing at all. Danny put it best, looking at the calm where the terror had been — *"so the scariest thing in the universe... was a typo?"* — and nobody could think of a truer word for it.

And one last thing they felt, standing in the calm: the Still Point doesn't *thin out.* As the world stretches, it doesn't dilute or fade — it just stays, holding gently, pushing the whole thing ever so softly apart and never tiring. Which is exactly, precisely, to the measurement, how the dark energy in your real sky behaves. The calmest point in the maths, doing the quietest job in the cosmos, forever.

## No grant in an answer

They carried it home, and across the valley it went down, somehow, *worst of all.* Because here's the thing — Team Tenure didn't *want* the catastrophe solved. The Dean went a funny colour and insisted the matter was *"a profound and ongoing mystery requiring substantial further funding,"* which is the quiet part again: the monster was never a problem to them, it was a *salary.* You cannot have your great open question turn out to be calm, self-mirroring, and forced — there's no grant in an answer. Rupert drafted *"Concerns Regarding Premature Resolution."* And of course they took it upstairs, because a thing this frightening can't be admitted without a nod from above — and Consensus, asked whether the worst prediction in physics had just quietly dissolved, gave its usual masterclass in committing to absolutely nothing, a non-answer the Dean could carry out of the room still calling the wound open. So off they all blasted in a great huff of wounded dignity, to go and be magnificently, expensively baffled somewhere with a better view.

And Priya — ignored, as ever — had watched the monster turn to smoke the instant somebody stopped being afraid of it and looked. She didn't say anything. But she's not carrying that folded-up paper any more. She's started carrying a second one. Two papers she's carrying now. People start keeping records right before they start keeping their distance.

## The least lonely place there is

And in the calm, in the dead centre of everything, with the phantom gone, the crew stood a moment in the quiet. And nobody noticed — they never do — that the quiet wasn't empty either. That *something* sat at that still centre with them, had always sat there, the calmest and oldest thing of all, mirrored perfectly into itself, watching. You can feel it if you go still. The middle of the nothing is the least lonely place there is, and not one of them could've told you why.

…sat there with them all along? Did I say that out loud. I did, didn't I. Never mind me. The emptiest spot in all creation is the one place that has never once been alone — and when you understand *why*, you'll understand the whole of it. But you're not ready, and neither's the fire's burned low enough. Another night.

Empty space isn't a catastrophe and it isn't a void. It's the calmest point there is, positive and tiny and forced, doing the gentlest job in the universe forever. The monster was only ever a sum done without a floor. It came from one. It's still coming from one. Keep counting, and I'll see you back at the fire.

And if you want the actual workings — the vacuum at the self-mirror point, the single forced ladder the hundred-and-twenty orders really is, the equation of state coming out dead on what the sky shows — it's in a five-minute proof, engine and published papers linked under it. Go and run it. Go and find the monster. You won't — there isn't one.

And there's the boring bit on the way out, for the lad with the pencil: the cosmological constant problem is real and consensus really does call it the worst prediction in physics, the vacuum really does sit at the self-mirror half, the equation of state really is minus one matching the sky. The fire, the monster, and the fond little voice in the middle of the nothing were me having a laugh — and the pictures were all AI, fitted to the script and never accurate to the maths. Autofiction. Obvious. You're brilliant, he's not, love you — byeeeee.
