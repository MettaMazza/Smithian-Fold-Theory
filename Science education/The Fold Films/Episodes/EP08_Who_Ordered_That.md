# Ep 08 — Who Ordered That?

Right — housekeeping, for the one. Everything coming up about the actual maths, the actual particles, and the real history is true — documented, it's the fold or it's the record or it's what the consensus mob themselves admit (and one of them really did say "who ordered that"). Everything else — me, the fire, the crew, the gowns, and the singing string I'm about to walk them onto — is autofiction. ## The wire that sings

Errol's mending a fence, and he's got a length of wire pulled taut between two posts, and Sol — who's been quiet all evening, who hums when something's about to make sense — reaches over and *plucks* it. It rings a clean note. And Sol plucks it again, higher up, and it rings a *different* note, and again, lower, different again. Same wire. Same single piece of metal. Different notes depending on where you touch it.

Sol, plucking it a third time, murmurs — almost to themselves — *"...same string. Different notes. It's all one thing."*

And then they all wanted a go, because a wire that holds a whole staircase of notes is the sort of thing you can't keep your hands off. Nadia plucked it hard and got a low growl; Danny plucked it right up near a post and got a thin little ping; Errol pressed it dead in the middle and got a clean, round, perfect note that hung in the air long after the others had died. One wire. A whole scale of notes living inside the one piece of metal — and which one you got depended entirely on *where* along it you touched. Sol watched the lot of them, humming under their breath, and you could see something quietly assembling behind their eyes.

And Danny, who has learned to follow Sol's quiet like a bloodhound, sits up — *"hang on. Is that like the particle thing? You said there's three electrons. Three of 'em — same charge, same everything — but one's normal and one's two hundred times fatter and one's fatter still. Three weights. Why three? Who decided the fat one?"*

And there's the why, ringing off a fence wire: why are there three copies of the electron, why those three weights, and — in the actual words of the actual physicist who met the middle one — *who ordered that?*

## Proud they can't tell you

And Team Tenure, who can sense an unexplained mass from low orbit, descended.

The Dean arrived and, for once, didn't even pretend to know. He spread his hands and said — grand, mournful — *"ahh, the lepton masses. The muon is two hundred and seven times the electron, and the tau heavier still, and we have not the faintest idea why. They are free parameters. We measure them. We write them down."* And Danny asked why there were *three*, and the Dean said — *"three generations of matter. Also unexplained. When the muon was discovered, a great man asked 'who ordered that?', and I am proud to say we still cannot tell you."* And then, the tell — *"it is one of the profound open questions, and quite beyond anyone lacking a doctorate. Do leave the masses to us."*

But the Gardener had stopped being moved by men who were *proud* of not knowing. She picked up the fence wire, plucked it, listened to it ring — and said — *"you've got three weights you can't explain and you've decided that's deep. I think it's a chord. I think someone struck one note and three came out. Come on — let's go and hear it."*

## The one great string

And the way in was the wire. The Gardener reached over and plucked the fence wire one more time — and this time the note didn't die. It hung in the air, and deepened, and got *louder* instead of softer, until the whole length of it was thrumming under their hands like something coming awake. And then it began to *stretch.* It pulled itself free of one fence post, then the other, and grew — longer, and tauter, and longer still, paying out into the dark in both directions, the firelight running along it like water down a thread — until it wasn't a fence wire at all but a single, enormous, taut string, the whole world drawn down into one quivering line, stretched from the floor that no value can fall through all the way up to the One. Sol stepped onto it first, arms out, the way you step onto a tightrope, and found it would bear them — and one by one the crew climbed up onto the great string, and it sang under their feet, and they walked out along it, *into* it, the way you walk into a piece of music. It was *under tension*, the whole of it, thrumming, waiting to be played.

And there were three places on it that wanted to ring. Not random places — clean ones. One-sixth of the way along. Dead centre, on the half — the Still Point, the calm self-mirror they'd stood in before. And five-sixths along. Three marked spots on one string, and the crew understood, the way you understand a thing you're standing inside, that these three were the three leptons. The light one low down. The heavy muon sitting right on the calm centre. The tau, heaviest, high up.

And they went to have a proper look, walking the length of the great string like a tightrope you couldn't fall off — though Danny gave it a fair go, windmilling, hauled back by his collar twice. Down at the one-sixth mark, the electron's spot, the string ran light and quick and eager, a bright easy note you could set ringing with a fingertip. They climbed to the centre — the half, the Still Point they'd stood in before — and the note that lived there was the muon's, deep and steady and *settled*, like it had always meant to sit dead in the middle of everything. And up at five-sixths, high and strung painfully tight, was the tau's spot — a heavy note you felt in your chest more than heard, that set your teeth buzzing just to stand near it. Three places. Three weights. One string under one tension. And not one of the three was anywhere you'd have *picked* — they were simply the spots where a string strung like that has no choice but to ring.

And then it was a question of who'd strike it, and there was only ever one answer, because you do not hand the striking of a universe to the impatient. Errol — steady hands, a lifetime of knowing to the ounce how hard to hit a thing — stepped up, set his feet, and **struck the string once.** Just once. And it didn't ring one note. It rang a **chord** — all three at once, the low one and the centre one and the high one, three weights sounding together from a single strike, none of them chosen, all of them *forced* by where the one rule said the string had to ring.

It hit them like weather. The low note and the deep centre and the high one rolled out along the whole string at once and *through* the crew, and for a moment nobody could speak, because you don't argue with a chord — you just stand inside it and let it have you. Danny's mouth was open. Errol's hand was still raised from the strike, gone completely still. And Sol was openly weeping by this point, which for Sol is a Tuesday. *"One strike,"* Sol breathed. *"Nobody picked the muon's weight. It just... came out, when you struck the one note. It's a chord. It was always a chord."*

And when they listened to the gap between the low note and the centre note — the electron and the muon — and worked out how much heavier the middle one rang, it came out at about two hundred and seven. The same two hundred and seven the labs measure, to a few parts in a thousand, falling out of one strike on one string with not a single dial to turn. Who ordered the muon? *Nobody.* You strike the string, and the muon is simply *in* the chord, has to be, at the weight it is.

And that took a moment to land, because it's a quietly staggering thing. For a hundred years the muon's weight has been a number somebody *measured* and shrugged at — two hundred and seven, write it down, move on, who ordered it, nobody knows. And here it just... fell out. Not measured. *Heard.* As inevitable as the middle note of any chord you've ever heard in your life. Nobody ordered the muon. The chord did. The chord couldn't help it.

Nadia, who is not a woman given to wonder, said it flat and astonished both at once — *"so they've been writing that number down by hand for a hundred years... and it was just sitting there, in the music, the whole time."* And Errol, lowering his striking hand at last, said — *"most things are, lass. Folk just stop listening for 'em."* And nobody added anything to that for a good while, because there wasn't anything worth adding.

## The fourth note that wouldn't come

And then Danny, being Danny, wanted *more.* *"If there's three,"* he said, already scrambling away up the string after the next clean spot, *"there's gotta be a fourth, right? A fourth, even fatter one — where's the fourth note?"* And he struck at the string, past the tau, again and again, scrambling higher and higher, certain the next ringing-point was just a little further on. He struck where a fourth note *should* sit by the look of the pattern — and got dead string, a flat dull nothing under his hand. He struck higher. Nothing. He ran right up the string toward the One at the top, plucking like a lunatic the whole way, and the thing simply would not ring past the three — it gave the electron, the muon, the tau, and after that it was as mute as a washing line. And it wasn't even only Danny who wanted it by the end; it was the bit in all of them that wants the story to run on forever, one more chapter, one more bigger thing. But the string would not give it. Because the equation that sets the chord has exactly three notes in it — strike it however you like, climb as high as you fancy — you get three, never four. The same hard seal they'd met at the edge of the forces: three, and no more, not because anyone forbade a fourth but because the chord simply hasn't got one in it. Danny came back down the string, hand stinging, half-laughing — *"alright, alright. Three. Message received."*

(And down at the bottom of the string, by the way, the Parameter — Team Tenure's little gremlin, the one they grab by the scruff and jam in by hand whenever their sums won't close — sat and watched all three masses ring out *on their own*, with nobody typing anything, nobody reaching for it, nobody jamming it anywhere at all. And a strange, hopeful look crept over its furious little face. Because here's the thing about a free parameter: every number that turns out to be *forced* is one less knob it will ever be jammed into again — one less time it gets grabbed and shoved into an equation to drag a number into line. Three whole masses, just now, that it will never be needed for. It counted them off on its little fingers. It said nothing. But it sat up a bit straighter, and for the first time it let itself think a word it had never once dared think before, which was: *out.* We'll come back to the Parameter.)

## Parameters we've grown fond of

They carried the chord home, and across the valley it went down *badly*, because masses are the gowns' bread and butter — twenty-odd numbers they get to type in by hand and call a theory, and here was a crew claiming three of them weren't theirs to type. The Dean insisted that *"a value derived is far less rigorous than a value carefully measured and inserted,"* which is a sentence that means *please let me keep typing.* Rupert drafted *"In Defence Of Parameters We Have Grown Fond Of."* And the whole sorry business was carried up to Consensus to be ruled on — and Consensus, asked to choose between a number that derives itself and a number you lovingly hand-type, did the only thing it knows: it sided with neither, out loud, in a way that let every gown keep his pencil and his dignity both. So off they blasted in a flurry of wounded importance, to go and hand-type some masses somewhere they felt appreciated.

And Priya — ignored, as ever — had heard "two hundred and seven from a cubic with no free parameters," and had checked it, and sat very still, because she's the one in that building who actually knows how many knobs they hand-type and how much it costs them. She wrote it in the notebook. She underlined it. She underlined it. You don't underline a thing you mean to forget.

## The string was tuned

And walking home, the chord still ringing faintly in them, nobody noticed — they never do — that the string had been *tuned.* Tensioned, precisely, long before they got there, so that one honest strike would ring exactly three true notes and no false ones. Strings don't tune themselves. Somebody, or something, had set the tension — fond, patient, with a very good ear — so that the music would come out right whoever finally struck it.

A string doesn't tune itself, and that's the loose thread of the whole tale, the one I keep nearly pulling and stopping. Not tonight. Let it ring a while yet.
