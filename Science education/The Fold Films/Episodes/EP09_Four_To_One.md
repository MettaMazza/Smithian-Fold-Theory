# Ep 09 — Four to One

Right — housekeeping, for the one. Everything coming up about the actual maths, the actual particles, and the real history is true — documented, it's the fold or it's the record or it's what the consensus mob themselves admit. Everything else — me, the fire, the crew, the gowns, and the three jumping-channels I'm about to drop them onto — is autofiction. And the pictures are AI, fitted to the script and never accurate to the maths or the story. Made up, to tell you something true. You knew that. Course you did. But there's the one who never clocked it was a story, bless his cotton socks, so: clocked. Hello. Sit down.

## You can't call it

Buzzing still off the chord from last time, and there's a small, vicious betting game going on by the fire — coins, mostly, and Nadia is cleaning Danny out, as is the natural order of things. It's a daft little game — you flip, you call it, you win or you lose your copper — and it ought to be pure chance, fifty-fifty, nothing in it to *know.* And yet Nadia has Danny's entire fortune in a neat stack by her knee, and Danny has a face like a slapped backside, because losing eleven on the trot stops feeling like chance somewhere around the eighth and starts feeling like a personal insult. And Danny, down to his last two coppers, slaps the ground.

Danny, scowling at the coin in Nadia's hand, says — *"you can't KNOW, though. That's the whole point of a bet. Nobody can call a thing before it happens. You just got lucky."*

And the Gardener, who's been half-watching from across the fire, says quietly, not even looking up — *"sometimes you can. If you know the rule the thing runs on, you can call it stone dead before it ever happens. That's not luck. That's the best trick there is."*

Danny didn't buy it. *"You can't, though,"* he insisted, picking at the dirt. *"Not for definite. Things just... happen, and then you find out after."* And the Gardener shrugged — *"a coin, maybe. Too many little nudges in a coin to track. But some things run on one clean rule. And a thing that runs on one clean rule, you can call right down to the bone."*

And Danny chews on that, and then it joins up with the thing still rattling round his head from the singing string — *"...the fat note. The heavy one. The tau. You said it doesn't last — it changes into the lighter ones. So when it changes — which one does it pick? The electron, or the muon? And — can you call it? Before it happens? For definite?"*

And there's the why, born off a coin and a leftover question: when a thing changes, can you bet the outcome in advance and *be right?*

## We never predict a ratio

And Team Tenure, who can smell a falsifiable question forming from a great distance and do not care for it, arrived.

The Dean swept in and explained, with enormous patience, that the tau *can* very rarely change flavour, and that they had built magnificent experiments to catch it doing so, and Danny asked the obvious — *"so which does it turn into more, the electron or the muon? What's the odds?"* — and the Dean recoiled as though slapped. *"We would never be so reckless as to* predict *a ratio,"* he said. *"We measure. And then, once we have measured, we explain — with great rigour — why it was always going to be that. To call a number* in advance *is the behaviour of a — "* and here he glanced, again, at Danny's trainers — *"an amateur."*

And the Gardener stood up. Because *that* — that right there — is the thing she despises most in the whole cold building. *"You never call it,"* she said, flat. *"You wait, you measure, then you say you knew all along. That's not science, that's a bookie who only takes bets after the race. We'll call it. In advance. Come on."*

## The coin that wouldn't land

And the way in was the coin. The Gardener took Danny's very last copper, to make her point, and flipped it — *"heads,"* she called, flat, before it had even left her thumb — and it went up spinning into the dark. And it didn't come down. It hung there, turning, and as it turned it *grew* — wider, brighter, a spinning disc the size of a cartwheel, then a door, then a wall of light — until the spin of it had scored three bright lines clean across the whole dark. Three glowing channels, evenly spaced. The electron channel, low, at a quarter. The muon channel, dead centre, at a half. The tau channel, high, at three-quarters. And the coin, still turning, drifted down and settled gently onto the middle channel and lay there, heads up — called right, before it ever flew. The Gardener stepped onto the channel after it, and the crew climbed up beside her — onto the three lines, where the heavy one was already waiting: up on the tau channel, restless, fizzing, plainly not long for this world, getting ready to jump.

They'd never seen anything like the tau up close. It didn't sit still for a heartbeat — it *fizzed*, crackling at its edges, too heavy and too full of itself to last, the way the heaviest things never do. Standing near it felt like standing near a struck match in a high wind: any second now and it'd be gone, *changed*, flung off somewhere lighter. Danny reached a hand toward it and Errol hauled him back by the shoulder — *"don't. You do not want to be stood on a channel when something jumps off it."*

Because that's what a flavour-change *is*, here — a jump from one channel to another. And this world had one iron rule about jumps, which Sol read off the air the moment they landed, going still — *"the rate of a jump goes as the* distance*. Squared. The farther the leap, the more often it happens. Squared."*

And that "squared" is the whole trick of the place, and it's a sly one, because it means the rule *rewards distance.* The farther a thing has to leap, the *more* eager the jump — which is backwards to everything your gut will tell you, and exactly the sort of thing a gut gets wrong and a rule gets right. Nobody quite clocked, yet, what that was about to do to their bets.

So the Gardener gathered them for the real bet — the proper one, the one that mattered — *"right. The tau's going to jump. Down to the electron, or down to the muon. Which way does it go more often? Put your money down."*

And Danny, who had learned absolutely nothing from the coins, went straight in — *"the muon! Obviously the muon. It's right next door, it's the closer one, the short hop's gotta be easier!"* And it is the most natural bet in the world, and Nadia put a coin on it too, and even Errol nodded along, because *of course* the near jump is easier.

And that's the lovely thing — the *whole crew* went near. Matthew, the muon. Frances, the muon, and she's the one who used to *teach* this. Even Sol, who'd read the rule off the air ten seconds before, hedged toward the near one, because the gut is *loud*, and it shouts *short hop, easy hop* clean over the top of anything a rule quietly whispers. Five of them, all certain, all on the muon, all about to be wrong. Only the Gardener hung back, doing the sum behind her eyes.

And the Gardener shook her head. *"No. Look at the rule. Rate goes as distance, squared. The electron's twice as far from the tau as the muon is. And twice the distance, squared, is four times the rate. It'll cross all the way to the electron — the* long *leap — four times as often as it hops to the muon. Bet on the far one."* Danny laughed in her face, fond as anything — *"twice as far is* harder*, not easier. Ask your legs."* — and slapped his last two coppers down on the muon, the near one, the sensible one. The Gardener didn't argue. She just said, *"watch,"* and put nothing down at all — because she wasn't *betting.* She was *calling.*

## The far leap wins

And they watched. The tau fizzed, and jumped — to the electron. Fizzed, jumped — electron again. Then the muon, once. Then electron, electron, electron, muon.

And the crew's faces did a wonderful thing, which was *fall*, slowly, all five at once, as their sensible near-bet kept losing. Every time the tau gathered itself they leaned in willing it muon-ward — short hop, short hop — and every time, four times in five, it ignored them completely and flung itself the whole long way to the electron instead, almost *gleeful* about it, like it preferred the big daft leap. Matthew groaned. Frances put her head in her hands — *she taught this* — and muttered that it was backwards, it made no sense. And the Gardener said gently — *"it makes perfect sense. It just doesn't make* gut *sense. The rule squares the distance. The far jump pays four times over, every single time."*

And they counted, and they counted, and it settled exactly where the rule said it would: for every one time the tau took the short hop to the muon, it took the long leap to the electron **four** times. Four to one. The far jump, the one that looks harder, winning easily — because the rule rewards distance, and squares it. Danny stared at his lost coppers, then started laughing — *"the FAR one. It likes the far one. That's mental. That's brilliant. How's that even — "* and Sol, quietly delighted — *"because rate goes as distance squared. The big leap pays four times over."*

And Danny went quiet then — the boy who'd opened the whole night swearing nobody can call a thing before it happens — looking from his empty hand to the tau, still flinging itself electron-ward, exactly as told. *"You called it,"* he said, almost accusing. *"Before it happened. You actually called it."* And the Gardener — *"told you. Know the rule, and it's not a bet at all. It's just reading on ahead."*

And then came the part that made this one different from everything they'd done before, and the Gardener got very still and serious about it. *"Here's the thing,"* she said. *"Nobody's measured this yet. The labs are still building the kit to catch it. So we don't get to check our answer against theirs — there isn't one." She picked up a stick and wrote it in the dirt, big: FOUR TO ONE. "So we do the thing they're too frightened to. We write it down* now*. Before anyone's measured it. No take-backs, no fiddling it later. Four to one, electron over muon. And we hand it to the whole world and say: there. That's our number. Go and measure it, and if it's not four to one, we were wrong, out loud, in front of everyone."*

That's the bet. Not a coin by a fire — a number, called in advance, with the stopwatch handed to the other side. The exact opposite of a bookie who only takes bets after the race.

And the crew felt the size of it, standing there in the dirt with FOUR TO ONE scratched between them, because writing a number down before the world has measured it is a genuinely terrifying thing to do. It's the one move you can never take back — the one that can make a fool of you in public, on the record, for good. Danny, who'd just lost his very last copper backing the wrong horse, looked at the words a long moment and said, a bit awed — *"so if they measure it and it comes out three to one, or five... we're done. Everyone sees."* And the Gardener nodded, not bothered in the slightest — *"aye. That's the deal, love. That's the only honest deal there is. You don't get to be right unless you were willing to be wrong where everyone could watch."* And that, more than any number, is the thing the gowns across the valley have never once in their lives been brave enough to do.

## Nowhere to hide

And across the valley, when word reached the Institute that a crew of nobodies had *committed to a number in advance*, Team Tenure were not angry. They were *frightened.* Because a number you've bet in public can be *wrong* in public, and that is the one thing the building is built to never risk. The Dean went pale and said — *"but — but what if it's not four to one? They've left themselves nowhere to hide!"* — entirely missing that having nowhere to hide is the *whole point.* Rupert drafted *"On The Inadvisability Of Specific Claims."* And they fairly *ran* the matter up to Consensus, desperate for cover — and Consensus, asked to bless the act of betting a falsifiable number out loud, was so appalled by the very idea of committing to anything that it managed not to commit even to its own disapproval, which the Dean took, gratefully, as licence to predict nothing ever again. So off they blasted in a cloud of affronted dignity, to go and *not* call something, somewhere safe.

And Priya — ignored, as ever — watched a crew of nobodies bet a falsifiable number to the world's face, and felt something she hadn't felt inside that building in twenty years, which was the simple clean wish to be *allowed to be wrong out loud.* She wrote FOUR TO ONE in the notebook. She didn't underline it. She just looked at it for a long time. She felt something tonight she hadn't felt in twenty years inside that building. It does not go back in the box.

## It knew the odds

And as the tau fizzed and leapt, over and over, four-to-one toward the far channel, nobody noticed — they never do — that something was keeping count alongside them. Not nervous about the bet at all. Calm, certain, fond — like it had known the odds since before there were channels to jump between, and was only pleased someone had finally bothered to call them out loud.

…known the odds *since before the channels*, mind. Since before there was anything to bet on at all. Who'd you have to be, to know a thing that early? Don't look at me like that. I've said too much again — I always do, this near the end of a night. There's an answer to that question, and it's the biggest one there is, and you'll have it. When you're ready. Off you go.

When a thing runs on a rule, you can call it before it happens — and the tau leaps to the electron four times for every once to the muon, because the rule rewards the distance, squared. We've bet it. The stopwatch is yours. It came from one. It's still coming from one. Keep counting, and I'll see you back at the fire.

And if you want the actual workings — the channels at a quarter, a half, three-quarters, the rate-goes-as-distance-squared, the whole forced spectrum the four-to-one sits in — it's in a five-minute proof, engine and published papers linked under it. Go and run it. Better still: go and *measure* it, and try to make us wrong.

And there's the boring bit on the way out, for the lad with the pencil: lepton flavour violation is real and hunted for, the rate really does go as the separation squared, and the fold really does bet tau-to-electron over tau-to-muon at exactly four to one, in advance, for the labs to check. The fire, the jumping channels, and the fond little scorekeeper were me having a laugh — and the pictures were all AI, fitted to the script and never accurate to the maths. Autofiction. Obvious. You're brilliant, he's not, love you — byeeeee.
