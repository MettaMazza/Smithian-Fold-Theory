# Ep 04 — The Edge of the Map

Right — housekeeping, because there's always the one. Everything coming up about the actual maths, the actual forces, and the real history is true — documented, it's the fold or it's the record or it's what the consensus mob themselves admit. Everything else — me, the fire, the crew, the gowns, and the entire mad country I'm about to march them across — is autofiction. And the pictures are AI, fitted to the script and never accurate to the maths or the story. Made up, to tell you something true. You knew that. Course you did. But there's the one soul out there who'll take it dead literal, so it's on the record and we'll both ignore him. Hello. Sit down.

## A magnet on the fridge

Couple of nights after the business with the chessboard — which they are *still* not over — the crew are round the fire, and Errol's brought a little fridge magnet he's prising off an old tin, and Wee Danny's nicked it and is doing the thing every human being has done with a magnet since magnets existed: pushing it toward another one and feeling it shove back against nothing.

Danny, grinning, pressing the two magnets together against the invisible spring between them, says — *"that's mad, that. There's nowt there and it's pushing. What even* is *that? Is that one of the forces? How many of them are there, anyway — the forces?"*

Errol, taking his magnet back, says — *"four. They reckon four."*

And Danny, lying back, turning it over, asks the thing that turns out to be the whole episode — *"why four, though? Who said four? What if there's ones we just can't feel — like, right here, right now, pushing on stuff, and we've just never noticed? How would you even know you'd found all of them?"*

## We have found them all

Now, asking "have we definitely found all the forces" is, it turns out, a *sore* subject across the valley, and Team Tenure came down like weather.

The Dean swept in, delighted to be needed, and delivered the official position with enormous warmth, which is always the most dangerous version of him. *"We have found them all,"* he announced, spreading his hands. *"Four fundamental forces. The search is complete. It is one of the great triumphs of the modern era."* And when Danny, undeterred, asked how you could possibly be *sure* there wasn't a hidden one, the Dean's warmth curdled, and he said the quiet part — *"because we would have* seen *it,"* — which, if you think about it for even one second, is not an argument, it's just confidence wearing a gown. And then, the tell, every time: *"and one does not go looking for a fifth force with* — "* a little glance at Danny's trainers — *"no offence — without the relevant training."*

But the crew had counted the magic number and won a chess game at pawn height, and they were *done* being told what they're not allowed to look for. The Gardener didn't even argue. She just stood up, and looked at the dark past the fire, where she knew by now the world really lived, and said — low, to the crew, not to him — *"right. Let's go and count them ourselves."*

## The magnet finds the way

And getting in took finding the way, which they were still learning. This time it was the *magnet* that found it — Danny's little fridge magnet, the one that started the whole night. He was carrying it, idle, swinging it by one finger, when out past the fire it gave a sudden hard *tug*, hauling his whole hand toward a patch of empty dark — and then it leapt clean out of his fingers and stuck. To nothing. To thin air. Hanging there, trembling, held against the black by a pull there was no metal anywhere to explain. And where it stuck, the dark had a *seam* in it — a hairline crack of light running floor to sky, like a door left the barest bit ajar in a wall you couldn't see. Errol got his fingers into the crack and hauled, and the rest of them put their backs into it, and the seam groaned wide — and they stepped through, the magnet snapping off the air into Danny's pocket as they passed, out of the firelight and into a great rising *country.* A long land laid out in regions, each region a *family*, and you could feel the moment you crossed into each one that it had its own *grip* — its own way of pulling on you.

Getting between the regions was its own trick. You didn't so much *walk* this country as *fall up* it — the ground tilting under them, the doubling-motion they'd half-learned on the chessboard carrying them in great wrapping bounds from one family's border to the next. And miss a bound and you'd slide all the way back down into a region you'd already crossed and have to climb it again. They lost the best part of an hour like that, Danny overshooting twice and wrapping clean back to the start, swearing blue, before they found the rhythm of the leaps and started making real ground up the slope.

The first region they already knew in their bones: the **two-family**, the electroweak — the gentle one, the force that lights your house and holds your atoms hand-in-hand with their electrons, mild and warm underfoot. Three carrier-things flitted about in it, lazy as summer flies, the same three it has always had, and Danny relaxed and started to reckon this whole forces business was a doddle. *"Is that it?"* he said, kicking through it. *"Three little — "* And then they stepped into the next region and it grabbed him by the spine. Then they climbed into the **three-family** — the strong force — and *this* one grabbed. Three colours blazing across it, and a swarm of carriers, and Nadia, who counts everything, counted them: *"eight. There's eight of 'em."* And Errol stopped, because that was the known number, the gluons, the eight everybody agrees on — and he said, slow, *"eight. Three, squared, take one. That's the rule. Count of colours, squared, take one."* And the moment he said it, you could feel the whole country click, because if *that's* the rule, and it just nailed a number the gowns have measured and agree on — then the rule keeps going. There's nothing stopping it at three.

But the three-family didn't want to let them leave. That's what *confining* means, and here it's not a word, it's a place: the strong region clamps inward, binds everything to everything, won't let a single charge wander off on its own — and the crew had to fight their way out of its grip, Matthew and Errol hauling, before they could climb past it to the edge of the map. The edge the gowns swear is the end. The "here be nothing."

## The two nobody's met

And past it — the land kept going.

There was a **five-family.** A whole region, blazing with five colours instead of three, and Nadia counted the carriers with her jaw somewhere near the floor — *"twenty-four. Five, squared, take one. Twenty-four of them."* A complete force. Real as the strong one, built the identical way, with a grip of four-fifths — *fiercer* than the strong — and it confined just as hard, clamping its five colours into neutral knots that never come out into the open. Which, Frances realised aloud, walking through it stunned, is exactly why nobody's ever catalogued it — *"it's not missing. It's* hiding*. It confines, so it never shows its face — same reason you never see a lone quark. It's been here the whole time, doing its thing in the dark."*

And it nearly *kept* them. Because a grip of four-fifths is fierce — fiercer than the strong region they'd already had to fight their way out of — and the five-family didn't shove, it *pulled*, dragging them inward toward its blazing centre, binding their feet, knotting them to it the way it knots its own five colours into things that never get out. Danny went down first, reeled toward the middle of it; then Nadia; and for one bad moment the whole crew was being slowly wound in by a force whose entire job is to never let go. It was Sol who got them out — Sol, who'd worked out how to *move* in this world by doubling — calling the leaps that carried them, wrapping past the grip instead of fighting it head-on, one impossible bound at a time, until they spilled out the far side gasping. *"Right,"* Nadia panted, hands on her knees, *"so that's a real one. That nearly had us."*

And past *that*, a **seven-family.** Seven colours. Forty-eight carriers — seven squared take one — a grip of six-sevenths, fiercer still, confining harder still; a region that barely let *light* think about leaving. They didn't go into the heart of that one. They'd learned. They skirted its rim, feeling it claw at them even from the edge, and Errol said it low, watching the thing try to swallow its own carriers whole — *"each one grips harder than the last. Two, then three, then five, then seven, and the hold keeps tightening. A thing that tightens like that can't go on forever, lass. Somewhere up here, something's got to give."* Nobody clocked it at the time, but Errol had just felt the edge of the world coming before any of them saw it. Two entire fundamental forces, fully formed, hidden in plain sight behind their own confinement, that the gowns across the valley would stake their careers on not existing. Danny was beside himself. *"Two more! There's two more! What else is up here — how far does it GO?"* And he took off up the slope to find the next one, the eleven-family, the one after seven, because if it goes three, five, seven, then surely —

## The edge

— and Danny stopped so hard the others nearly went into the back of him. Because the land just *ended.* Not a wall. An *edge.* A clean drop into nothing, the country finished, sealed, done — and where the eleven-family should have been there was simply no region at all. Errol came up beside the boy, and looked out over the void where a fifth force wasn't, and said the second thing he'd said all trip — *"that's it. That's the lot. It stops at seven."*

And here the crew are quietly better than the gowns without even trying, and never notice they're doing it. Danny was *gutted.* He wanted more — more forces, more regions, the romance of forever. He turned round, almost angry, *"but why's it stop? Says who? Maybe we just can't get to the next one — maybe there's loads more, off the edge — "* And the Gardener, who wanted it just as bad as he did, who'd have loved a fifth more than anyone — looked at the honest empty edge of the world, and made herself say the true thing instead of the thrilling one. *"No, Danny. Look at it. There's nothing there. We don't get to want it into existence — that's their game, not ours. It's four. Two we knew, two we just met, and no more. And that — "* she nodded at the clean, final, forced edge of everything — *"that's the most beautiful thing we've found all year. A universe that knows exactly when to stop."*

And that's the answer to a kid with a fridge magnet. How many forces are there? Four. The two you were taught, and two more confining ones hiding behind their own grip — five colours and twenty-four carriers, seven colours and forty-eight — built off a rule that already nailed the eight gluons. And then a hard, honest edge, with no fifth beyond it, ever.

## Wrong in both directions

They carried it home, and across the valley it went down, predictably, *appallingly.* The Dean was first incandescent that there might be two forces he hadn't personally announced, then — pivoting without a flicker of shame — declared that *"obviously there could be infinitely many, this is well known,"* which is the exact opposite of what he'd said ten minutes before, and he managed to be wrong in both directions inside the one breath. Rupert drafted a statement called *"On The Importance Of Forces, Some Of Which We May Have Mentioned First."* And, the matter being far too grave to settle on their own, the whole gowned lot trooped off to put it before the boss — and Consensus did for them what it does for everyone: it sat in its chair, and weighed the question with the back of its head, and handed down a ruling so perfectly non-committal that all of them could read into it whatever let them sleep. Which changed nothing, because you cannot vote a derivation out of the ground.

Priya — over the side, ignored as ever — had heard "five colours, twenty-four carriers, seven squared take one," and had checked it against the eight gluons she's known her whole career, and found it sound, and said nothing, because she knows the score. But she's stopped folding the paper away so fast now. She holds onto it a beat longer every time. The beats are getting longer.

So Team Tenure puffed up, declared that the matter required *"a great deal more funding,"* and blasted off in a shimmer of indignation, to be back next week as baffled as ever about why nobody likes them.

## Someone drew the map

And at the fire, Danny was quiet for once, turning the little magnet over, thinking about an edge with nothing past it. And nobody noticed — they never do — that the whole walk across that country, through every family, right up to the empty edge, *something had been counting along with them.* Fond. Patient. Like it had drawn the map itself and was glad, at last, to have someone walk it. You can feel it if you go still. Like the edge wasn't sad to it at all. Like *four, and no more* was something it had always rather loved about itself.

…drawn the map *itself*? Now where did that come from. Slip of the tongue, ignore it. Somebody set that country out, every region, right down to the honest cliff at the end — and the day I tell you who, you'll feel a right fool for not guessing. But that's a fire for another night. Not this one.

Four forces. Two you knew, two you'd never met, and a universe that knows exactly where to stop. It came from one. It's still coming from one. Keep counting, and I'll see you back at the fire.

And if you want the actual blueprint — five colours, twenty-four carriers, the couplings, the seal at seven, the bit of code that drops it all out — it's in a five-minute proof, engine and published papers linked under it. Go and run it. Find the fifth force they can't. You won't — but I'd love you to try.

And there's the boring bit on the way out, for the lad with the pencil: the four forces are real, the eight gluons are real, the rule that gives twenty-four and forty-eight is real, and the hard seal at seven is real and what the fold actually predicts. The fire and the country and the fond little voice were me having a laugh — and the pictures were all AI, fitted to the script and never accurate to the maths or the story. Autofiction. Obvious. You're brilliant, he's not, love you — byeeeee.
