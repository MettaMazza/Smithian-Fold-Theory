Start with the haunting, because it is real history and it is extraordinary. Wolfgang Pauli was one of the sharpest and most merciless minds in the history of physics — the man whose exclusion principle organizes every atom, who predicted the neutrino, who could demolish a wrong idea with a single sentence. And Pauli was obsessed with one hundred thirty-seven for the whole of his adult life. He could not let it go. He believed that until physics could explain that number from first principles, it had not really understood anything at all — that the fine-structure constant was the great unfinished business of the entire subject. He took the obsession so far that he worked with the psychologist Carl Jung on the meaning of numbers, chasing the idea that one hundred thirty-seven sat at some join between the structure of the physical world and the structure of the mind. He knew that the ancient mystics had attached meaning to it — that in the old Hebrew counting, the word for the received mystical tradition itself, Kabbalah, sums to exactly one hundred thirty-seven, and Pauli, the hardest-headed physicist of his generation, did not find that funny; he found it provocative. He used to say that when he died, the first question he would ask the Devil was the meaning of the fine-structure constant. And then the universe did something that no novelist would dare to write. In the winter of nineteen fifty-eight, Pauli was taken into the Red Cross hospital in Zurich, gravely ill. When his assistant came to visit, Pauli asked him whether he had noticed the number of the room. The assistant had. It was room one hundred thirty-seven. The number that had haunted Pauli's entire scientific life was the number on the door of the room he died in. He never came out. Make of it what you will — but the man spent fifty years certain that one hundred thirty-seven was trying to tell physics something, and physics never gave him the answer.

He was not alone, and he was not the strangest. Arthur Eddington — the astronomer who first confirmed Einstein's bending of starlight, a giant of his age — became so convinced that the inverse fine-structure constant had to be a whole number derivable by pure reason that he built an entire elaborate theory to produce it. When the best measurements of his day put the number near one hundred thirty-six, Eddington produced a long chain of reasoning that delivered exactly one hundred thirty-six. And then the measurements improved, and the number crept to one hundred thirty-seven, and Eddington calmly revised his "proof" to deliver exactly one hundred thirty-seven instead — which earned him, from his less reverent colleagues, the mocking nickname Arthur Adding-One. They laughed at him. But notice what Eddington was actually insisting on, underneath the failed arithmetic: he was insisting that this number is not an accident, that it cannot just be whatever the universe happened to dial in, that a real theory must derive it as a matter of structural necessity rather than measure it. He was right about that. He simply had no real machine to do it, so he faked one, and got laughed at, and the laughter taught a generation of physicists that trying to derive one hundred thirty-seven was the road to crankery. And so they stopped trying — and started measuring it instead, and calling the measurement an explanation.

And then there is Feynman, who was as deep inside the standard model as any human has ever been, and who refused to pretend the number was understood. Feynman called it one of the greatest mysteries in all of physics — a magic number, he said, that comes to us with no understanding by man. He said you might say the hand of God wrote that number, and we do not know how He pushed His pencil. He said that every good theoretical physicist should write that number on the wall and worry about it. That is not a mystic talking. That is the most formidable physicist of the standard-model era confessing, in plain words and in public, that his entire framework could not produce its own most important number — that the strength of electromagnetism, the thing that holds every atom in your body together, was a quantity his physics had to be handed, not a quantity his physics could work out. Pauli chased it into the mystics. Eddington chased it into numerology and ridicule. Feynman simply stood in front of it and admitted, honestly, that nobody knew. Three of the deepest minds of the century, and the most that the best of them could say was: here is a number we cannot explain, and it may be the deepest thing we do not understand.
