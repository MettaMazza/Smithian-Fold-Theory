# The Three-Body Problem Is Solvable — In the Right Numbers

For three hundred years the three-body problem has stood as the wall at the edge of physics. Two bodies under gravity — Earth and Sun, a planet and its moon — we mastered in the seventeenth century; the orbits are clean ellipses, predictable to the end of time. Add a third mass, let three bodies pull on one another at once, and the mathematics detonates. In the eighteen-eighties Henri Poincaré, attacking it for a prize offered by the King of Sweden, proved something that has haunted science ever since: there is no general closed-form solution. Worse, the motion is chaotic — so exquisitely sensitive that a hair's-width change in the starting positions sends the bodies into a completely different future. It is why we cannot predict the deep fate of the solar system. It is the problem a global television series was named for, because it has become the very symbol of the limit of what can be known.

But hold that against a fact you can look up tonight. Around Jupiter, three great moons — Io, Europa, and Ganymede — have circled in perfect, locked stability for billions of years. And their orbital periods are not random. They stand in a ratio of one to two to four. Io goes around once; Europa, in that same time, exactly twice; Ganymede, exactly four times. A flawless doubling, holding steady across the entire age of the solar system. So here is the paradox that should stop you cold: if three bodies under gravity are doomed to chaos, how are Jupiter's three moons the most stable clockwork in the sky, frozen into a one-two-four doubling for eternity? The Smithian Fold answers that paradox, and in answering it, it solves the three-body problem — not approximately, not numerically, but exactly, with a proof you can check on the back of your hand. The only price of admission is to use the right numbers.

First understand, precisely, why physics calls it unsolvable, because the reason is the entire secret. The chaos lives inside one assumption so deep that no one thinks to question it: that the positions of the bodies are real numbers — points on a perfectly smooth, infinitely divisible line, carried to infinite decimal precision. In that infinite continuum, two starting points differing in the trillionth decimal, or the trillion-trillionth, eventually diverge into separate histories. That is the butterfly effect, and it is the whole of the unpredictability: to forecast the system forever you would need its starting positions to infinite precision, and infinite precision is not a thing the world can give you. Poincaré was right. In the real numbers, the three-body problem has no solution. But across three centuries no one asked the question that breaks it open: what if the real numbers were never real — what if the smooth infinite continuum is a fiction we painted over a universe that does not contain it?

That is exactly what the fold says, and it does not say it idly — it forces it. The fold refuses zero and refuses the infinitely small, and the physics it builds inherits that refusal at the deepest level. In this theory spacetime is not smooth; it has a floor. When the theory builds a black hole, the singularity — the supposed point of infinite compression — is rejected outright, and a smallest physical distance appears in its place, a minimum spacing below which no separation exists. The metric of space itself comes in discrete grains. There is no trillionth decimal place of position, because there is a smallest length, and beneath it there is nothing to subdivide. The continuum is not merely unnecessary in the fold; it is forbidden. And once the continuum is gone, the positions of bodies are no longer infinite decimals — they are fractions on a grid, whole parts of the One, with bounded denominators. The infinite precision that fed the butterfly has been cut off at the source.

Now, the conservation law — because this is what guarantees the bodies can never escape into chaos, and it is the piece the surface version of this story leaves out. Ordinary mechanics has its conserved quantities: energy, momentum, things that cannot change as the system evolves. The fold has one too, and it is beautiful. When you fold a position — double it and cast out the whole — the odd part of its denominator does not change. A body at some number of sevenths, folded, is still at some number of sevenths; folded again, still sevenths. The denominator is a conserved invariant of the motion, as rigid as energy. And that single conservation law is the whole reason the three-body problem is tame: because the denominator cannot change, the bodies are confined forever to the finite grid of sevenths — and a system trapped on a finite grid, stepping by a fixed rule, has only finitely many places to be. It cannot wander off to infinity, cannot diverge, cannot find a new decimal to scatter into, because the conserved denominator has locked the entire dance onto a finite stage. On a finite stage, a moving system must eventually return to a position it has already occupied — and the moment it does, it repeats, forever. Finiteness plus conservation forces the cycle. There is no escape clause.

So watch it close, with nothing hidden. Place three bodies at one-seventh, two-sevenths, and four-sevenths. Apply the fold — double each, cast out a whole if you cross one. One-seventh doubles to two-sevenths. Two-sevenths doubles to four-sevenths. Four-sevenths doubles to eight-sevenths, which is past the whole, so cast out seven-sevenths and keep one-seventh. The three bodies are now at two-sevenths, four-sevenths, one-seventh — the same three places, passed around the ring. Fold again: four-sevenths, one-seventh, two-sevenths. Fold a third time: one-seventh, two-sevenths, four-sevenths — every body home, exactly, on the spot it began. Three folds, and the system has returned to itself perfectly, and so it will repeat for eternity. No chaos, no butterfly, no drift in a decimal that does not exist. The three-body problem, the wall of three centuries, is a waltz in three steps, and every step is a doubling of sevenths you can check yourself.

Now hear why it was exactly three, because the number is forced, and it carries the whole general theorem on its back. Folding a position with denominator seven is doubling its numerator and wrapping at seven: one, two, four, then eight which wraps to one. Three doublings to come home. Mathematicians have a name for that count — the order of two modulo seven — and they have known how to bound it since sixteen-forty, when Pierre de Fermat proved his Little Theorem: for any prime denominator, doubling returns to the start in a number of steps that always divides the prime minus one. For seven, that is six, and the true period is three, a divisor of six, exactly as Fermat guarantees. So the period of a three-body orbit is not a thing you discover by grinding a simulation for a billion years and praying it does not diverge. It is a thing you compute in seconds, by hand, from a theorem older than calculus. The grid of sevenths breaks into clean cycles — one-seventh, two, four spinning together in one ring of three; three-sevenths, six, five spinning in another — and every configuration of bodies you can place on the grid lands in one of these rings and circles forever. And all the while, the fold's gravity conserves what gravity must: mass and momentum are frozen invariants on the lattice, the lower moments forbidden from leaking away, so the cycle is not some mathematical curiosity disconnected from physics — it is a genuine orbit, obeying genuine conservation laws, that happens to close.

And none of this is special to three bodies or to sevenths. The theory proves the general case directly: any number of bodies, on any bounded-denominator grid, returns to its exact starting configuration in a fixed, finite, computable number of folds. A body at three-fifths cycles three-fifths, one-fifth, two-fifths, four-fifths and home in four steps — the order of two modulo five. Ten bodies, a thousand bodies, a million — the joint state lives on a finite grid, the conserved denominator pins it there, finiteness forces the return, and Fermat bounds the period. The general n-body problem, the thing every textbook files under hopeless, is periodic for every n, with a period you can write down before the motion begins. Not approximately. Exactly.

And now the universe steps in to confirm it, because this is not only a theorem — it is written in the sky. Return to Jupiter's moons. Io, Europa, Ganymede, locked in periods of one, two, and four — a doubling cascade, the precise signature of the fold, holding stable across the entire history of the solar system. The astronomers call it the Laplace resonance, and they have always treated it as a special, almost lucky configuration. The fold tells you it is not luck and not special — it is what three bodies do when they settle onto the grid: they fall into a doubling cycle, because doubling is the fold, and the fold is the motion. And the same fingerprint is everywhere once you look. Neptune and Pluto, locked two-to-three. The gaps in the asteroid belt, carved at the resonances with Jupiter. Orbital resonance — rational, locking, periodic — is not a rare accident in celestial mechanics. It is the rule, and the fold says it must be the rule, because every position is a fraction and every fraction folds into a cycle. The continuum predicted chaos and could not explain why the solar system is full of perfect rational locks. The fold predicts the locks, and hands you the chaos-free reason: there was never a continuum for the chaos to live in.

So where did the chaos go? It went home, to the infinite decimals it came from. The unpredictability of the three-body problem was never a fact about gravity — it was a fact about the smooth real numbers we insisted on using to describe gravity. Chaos is what infinite precision does when you let it diverge. Take infinite precision away — keep the world on the finite, conserved grid the fold builds — and chaos has no soil to grow in. It vanishes, and an exact, eternal periodicity stands where it stood. Poincaré proved there is no solution in the real numbers, and his theorem is untouched and forever true. The fold does not break it. The fold walks around it, by showing that the real numbers were a human overlay and the universe beneath them is woven from finite folds, where the same problem is not merely solvable but childlike. That is why three centuries of brilliant people could not crack it: they were searching for order inside infinite precision, and infinite precision has no order to find — only the butterfly. They were not failing at a solvable problem. They were succeeding, perfectly, at proving an impossible version impossible, while the possible version — the finite one, the folded one, the one Jupiter's moons have been demonstrating overhead the entire time — sat underneath, waiting.

And notice the last gift the fold leaves on the table. The very same theory that makes the orbits periodic also forces the room they orbit in to have exactly three dimensions — because stable orbits exist only when the dimension is below four, and three is the largest whole number that allows them. The fold solves the three-body problem and, in the same breath, explains why there is a three-dimensional space for three bodies to orbit in at all. One axiom, refusing infinity on its first page, hands you the stable three-dimensional room, the bodies that move through it, the conservation law that confines them, and the exact, computable, eternal cycle they trace — the cycle you can watch, tonight, in the steady one-two-four heartbeat of three moons around Jupiter. The wall was built of infinity. The fold never admitted infinity. There was never a wall to climb — only a continuum to set down, and beneath it, three bodies, dancing in a ring, as solvable as one and one.
